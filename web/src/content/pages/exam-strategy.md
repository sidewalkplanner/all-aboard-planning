> Knowing the material is most of the battle, but not all of it. This guide covers how the exam asks questions, how to pace 3.5 hours, and the reasoning patterns that separate the best answer from a merely true one. Read it early, then again the week before your exam.

## How the exam asks questions

The AICP exam is 170 multiple-choice questions in 3.5 hours, drawn from the nine domains of the content outline. Each has four options and one best answer. <!-- VERIFY: confirm the current question count, time limit, and format with APA. --> See [Exam info](/aicp/exam-info) for the official details, and [how the domains are weighted](/aicp/course).

You'll see several formats:

| Format | What it looks like | How to approach it |
|---|---|---|
| **Single best answer** | "Which tool most directly…" | Predict the answer before reading the options, then find the closest match |
| **EXCEPT / NOT / LEAST** | "All of the following are required EXCEPT…" | Treat each option as true or false; the odd one out is the answer |
| **Roman-numeral combinations** | "I. … II. … III. … IV. … A. I and II only…" | Judge each statement on its own, then eliminate every option containing a false one |
| **Sequencing** | "In what order should these steps occur?" | Anchor the first and last steps; the middle usually follows |
| **Scenario sets** | A paragraph followed by several questions | Read the question first, then return to the scenario for the facts it needs |
| **Exhibits** | A table, chart, or schedule | Read the question first so you know which row or column matters |
| **Calculations** | Density, FAR, location quotient, present value | Write the formula, convert units, and check that the answer is reasonable |
| **Ethics judgment** | A planner's dilemma | Use the five-step method (below) |

## Pacing 3.5 hours

170 questions in 210 minutes is about **74 seconds per question**. Most take less; calculations and long scenarios take more. A workable plan:

1. **First pass: answer everything.** Don't leave anything blank on the first pass. If a question will take more than about two minutes, pick your best answer, **flag it**, and move on.
2. **Check your pace against checkpoints.** Averaging about 67 seconds a question finishes the first pass with roughly 20 minutes left for review:

   | Time used | Aim to have answered about |
   |---|---|
   | 1 hour | 54 questions |
   | 2 hours | 108 questions |
   | 3 hours | 162 questions |

3. **Second pass: flagged questions only.** Return to what you flagged with fresh eyes.
4. **Change an answer only for a reason.** A misread question, or a fact you've since remembered, is a reason. A vague feeling isn't.

Practice this rhythm on at least one full-length timed practice exam in a single sitting. The practice exams let you flag questions and review them from a grid, just like this plan.

Never leave a question blank. As far as we know, there's no penalty for a wrong answer, so a blank is a guaranteed miss. <!-- VERIFY: confirm APA's current scoring policy on unanswered questions. -->

## Reading a question

- **Read the last sentence first.** It tells you what's actually being asked, so you read the scenario with a purpose.
- **Circle the qualifier** in your head: *first*, *best*, *most appropriate*, *primarily*, *most directly*, *EXCEPT*, *LEAST*. The qualifier decides between two otherwise correct options.
- **Notice who's acting.** Is the question about the planner, the planning commission, the elected body, or the courts? Many distractors describe the right action for the wrong actor.
- **Notice the stage.** Early in a process the answer is usually to gather information, define the problem, or engage people; late in a process it's usually to implement, monitor, or evaluate.

## The planner's answer

Many questions are judgment calls, and the exam rewards a consistent professional instinct. When you're between two options, the better one is usually the one that:

- **Follows the adopted plan and code.** Staff recommendations rest on adopted policy and approval criteria, not personal preference or public pressure.
- **Comes first in the process.** Analyze existing conditions before drafting goals; plan before you regulate; engage stakeholders before you present a finished solution.
- **Is legally defensible.** It rests on findings, a record, nexus and proportionality, proper notice, and consistency with the plan.
- **Includes people who are usually left out.** Targeted, accessible, trusted-partner outreach beats a single evening hearing.
- **Respects roles.** Staff analyze and recommend; appointed and elected officials decide; staff implement lawful decisions professionally.
- **Is honest and transparent.** Disclose, document, correct the record through proper channels.
- **Is proportionate.** It solves the problem at hand without overreaching, and without doing nothing.

## Answers that are usually wrong

- **True but irrelevant.** The statement is accurate but doesn't answer this question.
- **Absolute words.** "Always," "never," "all," and "only" are rarely right in planning, where most rules have exceptions.
- **Too passive.** "Wait and see," "let the commission decide without staff input," "ignore it."
- **Too aggressive.** "Go to the press," "file a charge immediately," "resign," or "deny the application" when the facts don't support it.
- **Outside the planner's authority.** Promising outcomes the planner can't deliver, or making decisions that belong to officials.
- **Right idea, wrong tool.** A variance when a rezoning is needed; a use variance where the code allows only area variances; mitigation when the question asks about adaptation.

## Working ethics questions

Ethics is about a tenth of the exam and follows predictable patterns. Use the five-step method from [Working through ethics scenarios](/aicp/lessons/solving-ethics-questions):

1. Who's acting, and in what role?
2. What's the issue: conflict, gift, confidentiality, honesty, competence, or pressure?
3. Is it a **Rule of Conduct** (enforceable) or an **aspirational principle**? Only a rule can be "violated."
4. Choose the **honest, proportionate** response that uses **proper channels**: disclose, withdraw, decline, correct the record, or raise it with the right person first.
5. Check it against the public interest.

Reliable instincts: decline gifts from anyone with a matter pending; with a financial conflict, disclose **and** withdraw; never alter accurate analysis under pressure; ask the Ethics Officer when genuinely unsure.

## Calculations without panic

A few calculation questions appear on most exams. They reward setup, not arithmetic skill.

1. **Write the formula** before touching the numbers. [Implementation math](/aicp/lessons/implementation-math) and the [quick reference](/aicp/review/quick-reference) list the ones that come up.
2. **Convert units first**: 43,560 square feet in an acre, and percentages as decimals.
3. **Estimate**, then compute. If the estimate and the answer disagree wildly, recheck.
4. **Use the options.** If two options differ by a factor of ten, the question is testing units.

## How to study so it sticks

- **Test yourself instead of rereading.** Recalling an answer strengthens memory far more than reading it again. That's why every lesson has checkpoint questions, and why the flashcards exist.
- **Space it out.** Short, frequent sessions beat long cramming sessions. Ten minutes of flashcards a day adds up.
- **Mix it up.** Once you've covered a few domains, mixed practice (the warm-up quizzes and full exams) trains you to recognize what kind of question you're facing, which is exactly what the exam demands.
- **Keep an error log.** For every miss, note *why*: didn't know it, misread it, fell for a distractor, or ran out of time. Each needs a different fix. The dashboard's "Lessons to review" list is a head start.
- **Take the diagnostic twice.** Once at the start to aim your study, and once midway to confirm your weak spots have moved.
- **Simulate the real thing.** At least one full-length exam, timed, in one sitting, at the time of day you'll test.

## The last week

- **Don't learn new material.** Review the [quick reference](/aicp/review/quick-reference), work your weakest flashcards, and reread the lessons your dashboard lists for review.
- **Take one last timed exam** early in the week, not the night before.
- **Confirm logistics**: appointment time, ID requirements, the testing site or remote-testing setup, and what you may bring.
- **Sleep.** A rested brain recalls more than a crammed one.

## On exam day

- Eat, hydrate, and arrive (or log in) early.
- Start with your pacing plan and trust it.
- If you hit a run of hard questions, flag and move. Difficulty isn't evenly spread, and a rough stretch doesn't mean you're failing.
- Some questions are unscored pretest items, and you can't tell which. Don't let one strange question rattle you. <!-- VERIFY: confirm APA still includes unscored pretest items. -->
- Use your review time on flagged questions, and don't second-guess answers you were confident about.
