## Learning objectives

- Put the plan hierarchy (vision, goal, objective, policy, action) in order and explain what each level does.
- Write a measurable objective and tell it apart from a goal.
- Draft clear policy language and choose directive words deliberately.
- Apply an equity lens to goals, objectives, and actions.
- Set priorities and translate vague values, such as "character," into measurable standards.

## Key concepts

### The plan hierarchy

Plans move from the general to the specific. The terms vary a little from plan to plan, but the logic doesn't:

| Level | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Vision** | A shared, aspirational description of the community's desired long-range future | "In 2045, Riverton is a place where people of every age and income can live near their work, their schools, and the river." |
| **Goal** | A broad statement of a desired end state; not directly measurable | "Housing choices meet the needs of residents at all income levels." |
| **Objective** | A **specific, measurable**, usually time-bound target that moves toward a goal | "Permit 2,000 new homes affordable to households below 80% of area median income by 2035." |
| **Policy** | A statement of the course of action the jurisdiction commits to follow when it makes decisions | "The city shall allow duplexes and triplexes in all residential districts." |
| **Action (implementation measure)** | A specific task with a responsible party, timeline, and resources | "Planning Department: draft zoning amendments allowing duplexes and triplexes citywide; present to Council by June 2026." |

A quick test: **a goal is broad, an objective is measurable, and a policy guides decisions.** "Improve walkability" is a goal. "Increase the share of residents living within a ten-minute walk of a park from 60% to 80% by 2035" is an objective.

### Writing good objectives

Objectives are how you'll know whether the plan is working. Many planners use the **SMART** test: **S**pecific, **M**easurable, **A**chievable, **R**elevant, and **T**ime-bound. A good objective:

- names a **metric** that can be tracked with available data;
- sets a **baseline** and a **target**;
- sets a **date**; and
- clearly supports a goal.

Objectives connect directly to plan monitoring. See [Implementation programs, partnerships, and monitoring](/aicp/lessons/monitoring-and-implementation-programs).

### Drafting policy

Policies are applied again and again: in staff reports, rezonings, capital budgets, and design reviews. They need to be clear enough that two planners reading them would reach the same conclusion.

- **Choose directive words deliberately.** "Shall" or "will" commits the jurisdiction; "should" states a strong expectation with room for judgment; "may" or "encourage" is permissive. Using "encourage" everywhere produces a plan that commits to nothing.
- **One idea per policy.** Long compound policies are hard to apply.
- **Avoid undefined terms.** "Compatible," "appropriate," and "high-quality" invite argument unless the plan says what they mean.
- **Stay within authority.** Don't write policies the jurisdiction has no power to carry out.
- **Keep the logic visible.** Each policy should trace up to a goal and down to actions.

### Visioning versus goal-setting

A **visioning process** asks participants to describe the community's desired **long-range future condition**: what it will be like to live there. A goal-setting workshop turns that picture into specific, organized statements of intent. Visioning comes first and is deliberately broad; it builds a shared picture before debating the details.

### An equity framework

An **equity lens** examines how the benefits and burdens of a policy are distributed, and who's been included in shaping it. Applied to plan writing:

- **Disaggregate the data.** Look at outcomes by neighborhood, race and ethnicity, income, age, disability, and tenure, not just citywide averages.
- **Ask the equity questions** for each policy: Who benefits? Who's burdened? Who was involved in writing it? What are the unintended consequences? How will we know?
- **Set measurable objectives for disparities.** To address the legacy of redlining meaningfully, a plan should set measurable objectives for disinvested neighborhoods, such as tree canopy, sidewalk completeness, and access to parks, rather than simply acknowledging the history.
- **Target resources.** Design universal goals but reach them with strategies tailored to groups that are furthest from them. This approach is sometimes called "targeted universalism." <!-- VERIFY: "targeted universalism" is commonly attributed to john a. powell; confirm before attributing. -->
- **Track and report** progress on those disparities over time.

### Priorities

Plans usually contain more actions than a community can fund at once. **Priority statements** and phasing tell implementers what to do first. Common criteria: the degree of impact on key goals, urgency (safety, legal deadlines), cost and available funding, readiness, equity, and whether an action unlocks others.

### Measuring "character"

When residents want to "preserve rural character," the planner's job is to **translate that value into measurable standards** without dismissing it. What does rural character mean here? Views of open fields from the road? Low building coverage? Narrow roads without curbs? Farm operations? Once named, each quality can become an objective and a policy, such as a maximum lot coverage, a scenic road setback, or a conservation subdivision standard.

## Key terms

- **Vision**: A shared description of the community's desired long-range future.
- **Goal**: A broad statement of a desired end state.
- **Objective**: A specific, measurable, time-bound target that advances a goal.
- **Policy**: A statement of the course of action a jurisdiction will follow in making decisions.
- **Action (implementation measure)**: A specific task with an assigned party, timeline, and resources.
- **SMART objective**: One that is specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound.
- **Baseline**: The measured starting value an objective is compared against.
- **Equity lens**: A set of questions examining who benefits, who is burdened, and who is included.
- **Disaggregated data**: Data broken out by subgroup or geography rather than reported only as a total.
- **Priority statement**: A statement ranking or sequencing actions.

## Real-world examples

**Rewriting a vague plan.** A city's old plan had 140 policies beginning with "encourage." None could be tracked, and developers and neighbors cited them on both sides of every rezoning. The update reorganizes the plan around 12 goals, each with two to four measurable objectives and a small set of "shall" and "should" policies, plus an action table assigning each task to a department.

**An equity objective.** Instead of the goal "improve parks in underserved areas," a plan update sets an objective: "By 2032, every household in census tracts with poverty rates above 25% will be within a 10-minute walk of a park, up from 58% in 2024." It pairs the objective with actions: acquiring two sites, adding a park to a planned school, and opening schoolyards after hours.

**Turning values into standards.** Residents of a rural township say they want to "keep it rural." Workshops reveal the specific concerns: views of farmland from county roads, large lots, and dark skies. The plan adopts objectives and policies for a scenic corridor setback, conservation subdivisions that preserve 50% open space, and outdoor lighting standards.

## Summary

Plans flow from vision (the shared picture) to goals (broad end states), objectives (measurable, time-bound targets), policies (commitments that guide decisions), and actions (specific tasks with owners and deadlines). Write SMART objectives with baselines, and write policies with deliberate directive words, one idea each, within the jurisdiction's authority. Apply an equity lens: disaggregate data, ask who benefits and who's burdened, and set measurable objectives for disparities. Set priorities so implementers know what comes first, and translate values like "character" into standards you can measure.
