## Learning objectives

- Distinguish primary from secondary data and quantitative from qualitative methods, and pick the right one for a planning question.
- Explain what the decennial census and the American Community Survey (ACS) each do well, and when to use each.
- Describe common sampling methods and the biases that undermine survey results.
- Explain the difference between validity and reliability, and use triangulation to strengthen findings.
- Recognize the ethical duties that come with collecting data about people.

## Key concepts

### Start with the question, not the data

Good planning research starts with a clearly stated question: *How many housing units will the city need by 2040? Why do residents avoid the new park? Which intersections have the most serious crashes?* The question decides what data you need, how precise it must be, and which method fits. A common mistake, and a common exam trap, is to reach for a familiar dataset or method before deciding what you're actually trying to learn.

A research design spells out:

- the question and the decision it supports;
- the unit of analysis (people, households, parcels, census tracts, trips);
- the data sources and how they'll be collected;
- the method of analysis; and
- the limits of what the results can tell you.

### Primary and secondary data

**Secondary data** already exists because someone else collected it: census tables, permit records, crash databases, assessor files, or another agency's survey. It's cheap and fast, but it was built for another purpose, so check its definitions, geography, and date before relying on it.

**Primary data** is collected by you for your question: a survey, a traffic count, a windshield survey of building conditions, interviews, or focus groups. It's tailored to your need but costs time and money, and its quality depends entirely on your design.

Most planning studies combine the two. For example, you might pair ACS income data (secondary) with a survey of local businesses (primary).

### Quantitative and qualitative methods

**Quantitative** methods measure and count: how many, how much, how often. They support statistical comparison and projection.

**Qualitative** methods explore meaning and experience: why people behave as they do, what a place means to them, how a process feels from the inside. Interviews, focus groups, participant observation, and open-ended survey questions are qualitative. If you want to understand residents' *lived experience* of displacement, a count won't tell you. You need to talk with people.

**Triangulation** means checking a finding with more than one method or source. If a survey shows low park use and focus groups explain that residents feel unsafe walking there after dark, the two methods reinforce each other and point toward a solution.

### The census and the American Community Survey

The **decennial census** is a complete count of the population every ten years. Its core purpose is apportioning seats in the U.S. House of Representatives, and it collects a short list of basic items (age, sex, race, Hispanic origin, household relationship, and tenure). Because it's a full count, it's the benchmark for population totals.

The **American Community Survey** is a continuous *sample* survey run by the Census Bureau. It collects the detailed social, economic, and housing characteristics the census no longer asks about, such as income, commuting, education, and housing costs. Because it's a sample, every ACS estimate has a **margin of error**.

- **1-year estimates** are the most current but are published only for larger geographies (areas of 65,000 people or more).
- **5-year estimates** pool five years of responses, are available down to census tracts and block groups, and are the standard choice for small areas. They describe a *period*, not a single year.

### Sampling

You can rarely survey everyone, so you survey a sample and generalize. How you choose the sample decides whether you can generalize at all.

- **Probability sampling** gives every member of the population a known, nonzero chance of selection. Only probability samples support statistical inference about the whole population.
  - *Simple random sampling:* every member has an equal chance.
  - *Stratified random sampling:* divide the population into subgroups (strata), such as renters and owners, then sample within each. This guarantees each group is represented.
  - *Cluster sampling:* randomly select groups (for example, blocks), then survey within them. It's cheaper but less precise.
  - *Systematic sampling:* pick every *k*th unit from a list after a random start.
- **Nonprobability sampling** includes convenience samples, snowball samples, and **self-selected** samples, such as an online survey open to anyone who wants to respond. These are useful for engagement and idea generation, but you can't treat the results as representative.

### Bias and error

- **Coverage (sampling-frame) bias:** some people can't be selected. A park survey done only on weekday mornings misses everyone who works then.
- **Self-selection bias:** people who choose to respond differ from those who don't. Public meeting attendees skew toward people with the time, transportation, and motivation to attend.
- **Nonresponse bias:** selected people who don't respond differ systematically from those who do.
- **Question-wording bias:** a **leading question** ("Don't you agree the new bike lanes are dangerous?") pushes respondents toward an answer.
- **Implicit bias:** the researcher's unconscious assumptions shape which questions get asked, how categories are defined, or how results are read. Pre-testing a survey with a diverse group of residents helps catch this.

### Validity and reliability

- **Validity** asks whether you're measuring what you intend to measure. Counting library cards issued is a weak measure of how much people read.
- **Reliability** asks whether the measure gives consistent results when repeated. A traffic counter that reports wildly different totals on identical days isn't reliable.

A measure can be reliable without being valid: a scale that is always five pounds off is consistent but wrong. It can't be valid without being reasonably reliable.

### Metadata and data quality

**Metadata** is data about data: who collected it, when, how, at what geography, with what definitions and known limits. Before using any dataset, read the metadata. It's how you find out that a "housing unit" field excludes group quarters, or that a crash database only includes crashes with police reports.

### Research ethics

When you collect information from people, you owe them informed consent, confidentiality, and a clear explanation of how their information will be used. This matters most when data is sensitive, as in health, immigration status, or income, or when participants are vulnerable. Store identifiable data securely and report results in ways that don't reveal individuals.

## Key terms

- **Primary data**: Data you collect yourself for a specific question.
- **Secondary data**: Existing data collected by someone else for another purpose.
- **Decennial census**: The complete count of the U.S. population taken every ten years.
- **American Community Survey (ACS)**: The Census Bureau's continuous sample survey of detailed population and housing characteristics.
- **Margin of error**: The range around a sample estimate within which the true value likely falls, at a stated confidence level.
- **Probability sample**: A sample in which every member of the population has a known, nonzero chance of selection.
- **Stratified random sample**: A probability sample drawn separately from defined subgroups to ensure each is represented.
- **Self-selection bias**: Distortion caused when respondents choose whether to participate.
- **Leading question**: A survey question worded to suggest a preferred answer.
- **Validity**: The degree to which a measure captures what it is meant to capture.
- **Reliability**: The consistency of a measure across repeated use.
- **Triangulation**: Confirming a finding with multiple methods or sources.
- **Metadata**: Documentation describing a dataset's origin, definitions, and limits.

## Real-world examples

**A neighborhood housing study.** A city wants to know whether rents are pushing out long-time residents of one neighborhood. Staff pull ACS 5-year estimates of rent burden by tract (secondary, quantitative), note that the margins of error are large, and aggregate adjacent tracts to get more reliable figures. They then hold interviews with tenants and community organizations (primary, qualitative) to learn how displacement is actually happening. Together, the two sources tell a stronger story than either would alone.

**A park-use survey gone wrong.** A parks department posts an online survey and gets 900 responses, most from nearby homeowners. The results favor adding a dog park. Because the sample is self-selected, the department can't claim this reflects the whole community. It follows up with intercept surveys at the park at different times and days, stratified by weekday and weekend, and translated into the neighborhood's most common languages.

**Testing a survey before launch.** Before a citywide transportation survey, staff pre-test the questionnaire with a small, diverse group. They discover that the question "How often do you use active transportation?" confuses people, and that the survey has no option for people who can't drive. Both problems are fixed before launch.

## Summary

Planning research starts with a clear question and a design that fits it. Secondary data is fast but built for other purposes; primary data fits your need but has to be designed carefully. Use the decennial census for complete counts and the ACS for detailed characteristics, choosing 5-year estimates for small areas and always minding the margin of error. Only probability samples can be generalized to a population, and every survey must guard against coverage, self-selection, nonresponse, and wording bias. Aim for measures that are both valid and reliable, confirm important findings through triangulation, and treat the people behind the data with care.
