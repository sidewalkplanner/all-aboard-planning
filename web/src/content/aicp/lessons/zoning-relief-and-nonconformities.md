## Learning objectives

- Distinguish area and use variances and apply the standard tests for granting a variance.
- Explain the difference between legislative and quasi-judicial rezonings, including the *Fasano* doctrine.
- Recognize spot zoning and distinguish contract zoning from conditional zoning.
- Explain how nonconforming uses, structures, and lots are treated, including amortization and abandonment.
- Describe what makes a development moratorium legally defensible.

## Key concepts

Zoning can't anticipate every property. The ordinance therefore provides ways to adjust the rules for specific situations, and ways to handle development that was legal before the rules changed. The exam tests these tools heavily.

### Variances

A **variance** is permission to depart from the ordinance's literal terms because applying them to a particular property would cause undue hardship. Variances are **quasi-judicial** decisions, usually made by the **board of zoning adjustment (appeals)** after notice and a hearing.

**Two kinds:**

- An **area (dimensional) variance** relaxes a dimensional standard such as a setback, height, lot coverage, or parking requirement. Many states apply a lower standard for these, sometimes called "practical difficulty."
- A **use variance** allows a use the district doesn't permit. It requires the stricter "**unnecessary hardship**" standard, and **many states and ordinances prohibit use variances entirely**, because allowing new uses is a legislative decision better handled by rezoning.

**Typical findings required** (terms vary by state):

1. The hardship arises from **conditions unique to the property**, such as an irregular shape, steep topography, or an unusually shallow lot, not from conditions common to the neighborhood.
2. The hardship **was not self-created** by the owner (buying a lot knowing it's substandard is often treated as self-created, though states differ).
3. Strict application would deny **reasonable use** of the property. **Financial hardship alone**, such as the ability to earn more money, isn't enough.
4. The variance **won't alter the essential character** of the area or harm neighbors or the public.
5. It's the **minimum variance** necessary to relieve the hardship.

### Rezonings: legislative or quasi-judicial?

In most states, a rezoning is a **legislative** act. Courts defer to it heavily, presuming validity and upholding it if the question is "fairly debatable."

In ***Fasano v. Board of County Commissioners*** **(Oregon, 1973)**, the Oregon Supreme Court held that a **site-specific rezoning**, which applies policy to a particular property rather than making general policy, is **quasi-judicial**. That means:

- the applicant bears the burden of proving the change is justified;
- the change must be consistent with the comprehensive plan;
- parties get procedural protections: notice, a hearing, an impartial tribunal, and a record; and
- the decision needs **written findings**.

### Spot zoning

**Spot zoning** is the rezoning of a small parcel to a classification inconsistent with the surrounding pattern and the comprehensive plan, **primarily for the benefit of the owner** rather than the public. Courts look at:

- the size of the area rezoned;
- **consistency with the comprehensive plan**;
- **compatibility** with surrounding uses; and
- whether the **public benefits**, or only the owner.

A small-parcel rezoning isn't automatically illegal. It's defensible when it implements the plan and serves a public purpose. The vice is singling out one parcel for special treatment.

### Contract zoning and conditional zoning

- **Contract zoning** is a bilateral bargain: the government promises to rezone in exchange for the owner's promises. It's widely held **invalid** because a government **can't bargain away its police power** or commit future legislative decisions.
- **Conditional zoning** attaches conditions to a rezoning, often proffered voluntarily by the applicant or imposed unilaterally by the governing body under ordinance authority (limiting uses, requiring buffers). Many states accept conditional zoning when it's authorized and serves legitimate planning purposes.

### Nonconformities

When zoning changes, or land is **annexed** into a city with different rules, some existing development no longer complies. If it was legal when established, it becomes a **legal nonconforming** ("grandfathered") use, structure, or lot, and **may generally continue**.

- **Nonconforming use**: a use no longer allowed in the district (a salvage yard now zoned residential).
- **Nonconforming structure**: a building that doesn't meet current dimensional standards (a house closer to the street than today's setback).
- **Nonconforming lot**: a lot smaller or narrower than the current minimum.

Ordinances typically aim for nonconformities to fade away over time:

- they **can't be expanded or intensified**;
- they **can't be rebuilt** if destroyed beyond a set percentage (often 50% of value);
- they can generally change only to a **conforming** use, or in some codes a less intensive one; and
- if **abandoned or discontinued** for a set period (often 6 to 12 months), the right to continue is lost.

**Amortization** requires a nonconforming use to **cease after a set period** that's meant to let the owner recoup their investment, with no compensation. Many state courts accept amortization if the period is reasonable given the investment and the harm the use causes; some states reject it. It's most often applied to billboards and to incompatible uses like junkyards or adult businesses.

### Moratoria

A **development moratorium** temporarily halts certain approvals while the jurisdiction studies and adopts new regulations. Courts uphold moratoria that are:

- **limited in duration** and **scope**;
- tied to an **identified regulatory purpose**, such as completing a plan, adding infrastructure capacity, or responding to a hazard; and
- pursued diligently, with a real work plan.

Under *Tahoe-Sierra* (2002), a moratorium isn't a per se taking. See [Takings, exactions, and property rights](/aicp/lessons/takings-and-exactions).

## Key terms

- **Variance**: Permission to depart from the ordinance's literal terms because of hardship unique to a property.
- **Area (dimensional) variance**: A variance from a dimensional standard.
- **Use variance**: A variance allowing a use not permitted in the district; prohibited in many states.
- **Unnecessary hardship**: The standard requiring that strict application would deny reasonable use because of the property's unique conditions.
- **Self-created hardship**: Hardship caused by the owner's own actions, which usually bars a variance.
- **Quasi-judicial rezoning**: A site-specific rezoning treated as applying policy, requiring findings and due process (*Fasano*).
- **Spot zoning**: Singling out a small parcel for inconsistent zoning mainly to benefit its owner.
- **Contract zoning**: A bilateral rezoning bargain, generally invalid.
- **Conditional zoning**: A rezoning with conditions attached, accepted in many states.
- **Legal nonconforming use**: A use lawfully established before current rules that no longer conforms.
- **Amortization**: Requiring a nonconforming use to end after a period meant to allow recovery of investment.
- **Abandonment**: Discontinuing a nonconforming use for a set period, ending the right to resume it.
- **Moratorium**: A temporary halt on specified development approvals.

## Real-world examples

**A proper area variance.** A homeowner's lot is wedge-shaped and slopes steeply at the back. Meeting the required rear setback would leave almost no buildable space for a modest addition. The board grants a five-foot rear setback variance, finding that the hardship comes from the lot's unique shape and topography, wasn't self-created, and that the addition won't affect neighbors' light or privacy.

**A variance that shouldn't be granted.** An owner asks for a height variance to add a fifth story because the project would be more profitable. The site is flat and typical of the district. Financial gain isn't a hardship and nothing about the property is unique, so staff recommend denial and note that a text amendment or rezoning is the proper route if the city wants taller buildings there.

**Annexation and a salvage yard.** A county salvage yard is annexed into a city that zones the area residential. The yard becomes a legal nonconforming use and may continue. To phase it out without paying compensation, the city adopts an amortization schedule giving the owner a reasonable period to relocate, based on an analysis of the owner's investment.

**Spot zoning risk.** A council is asked to rezone one lot in the middle of a single-family neighborhood to allow a gas station, contrary to the plan's designation. Staff warn that the rezoning would benefit only the owner, conflict with the plan, and be incompatible with its surroundings, and that a court could strike it down as spot zoning.

## Summary

Variances, decided by the zoning board, relieve hardships unique to a property that weren't self-created and aren't merely financial. Area variances are common; use variances are restricted or banned in many states. Most rezonings are legislative, but under *Fasano* site-specific rezonings are quasi-judicial, with findings and due process. Spot zoning singles out a parcel for the owner's benefit against the plan; contract zoning bargains away the police power and is generally invalid, while conditional zoning is widely accepted. Legal nonconformities may continue but can't expand, may be lost through abandonment, and may be phased out by reasonable amortization. Moratoria should be limited, purposeful, and temporary.
