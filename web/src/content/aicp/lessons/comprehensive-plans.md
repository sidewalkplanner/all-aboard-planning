## Learning objectives

- Define a comprehensive plan and explain how it differs from a zoning ordinance.
- List the elements commonly found in comprehensive plans, and explain what makes a plan internally consistent.
- Explain the consistency doctrine and the range of legal weight plans carry across states.
- Distinguish comprehensive, area, functional, regional, and strategic plans, and choose the right one for a situation.
- Describe how community character and form are addressed in a plan.

## Key concepts

### What a comprehensive plan is

A **comprehensive plan** (also called a general plan or master plan) is a community's **long-range policy guide** for its physical development. It's:

- **comprehensive**: it covers the whole jurisdiction and all the topics that shape physical development;
- **long-range**: it typically looks 20 years ahead;
- **general**: it sets direction and policy rather than lot-by-lot rules; and
- **officially adopted** by the governing body after public hearings.

A comprehensive plan is **not a regulation**. The **zoning ordinance** is the regulation that implements the plan. The plan says where the community wants housing, jobs, parks, and infrastructure; the zoning code sets the rules that apply to each parcel today.

A key tool inside the plan is the **future land use map**, which shows the intended pattern of land uses over the plan horizon. It's often confused with the **zoning map**, which shows the regulations in force now. The two will differ where the plan anticipates change.

### Common elements

State law often requires certain elements; communities add others. Common ones include:

| Element | What it covers |
|---|---|
| **Land use** | The future land use map and policies for the location, type, and intensity of development |
| **Transportation (circulation)** | Streets, transit, walking, biking, freight, and parking |
| **Housing** | Needs, supply, affordability, and strategies |
| **Economic development** | Jobs, business districts, and the tax base |
| **Natural resources and environment** | Water, habitats, air, and open space |
| **Parks and recreation** | Park needs, levels of service, and trails |
| **Public facilities and services** | Water, sewer, stormwater, schools, and public safety, coordinated with the capital improvements program |
| **Hazards and resilience** | Flooding, wildfire, climate adaptation, and hazard mitigation |
| **Historic and cultural resources** | Preservation priorities |
| **Community design and character** | Urban form, design principles, and gateways |
| **Implementation** | Actions, responsible parties, timelines, and funding |

Newer plans often add elements on **health, equity, climate and energy, food systems**, or **sustainability**. A **capital improvements element** connects plan policies to the facilities and funding needed to carry them out. See [Capital planning and public finance](/aicp/lessons/capital-planning-and-finance).

A plan should be **internally consistent**: its elements must work together. A housing element that calls for more homes shouldn't be undercut by a land use map with no room for them.

### Sustaining places

APA has promoted a set of standards for comprehensive plans built around sustainability principles: a livable built environment, harmony with nature, a resilient economy, interwoven equity, a healthy community, and responsible regionalism, supported by authentic participation and accountable implementation. ### How much legal weight does the plan carry?

The Standard State Zoning Enabling Act required zoning to be "in accordance with a comprehensive plan," but didn't define the plan. States have interpreted this in different ways, along a spectrum:

- At one end, courts treated the zoning ordinance itself (or its internal logic) as satisfying the requirement, so a separate plan wasn't needed.
- In the middle, the plan is **one factor** courts weigh when reviewing zoning decisions.
- At the other end, **consistency states** require zoning and development approvals to **conform to the adopted plan**. There, the plan supplies the policy basis on which zoning is justified, and an inconsistent rezoning can be invalid.

Oregon, Florida, and California are frequently cited as strong consistency states. <!-- VERIFY: confirm which states to cite as consistency states (Oregon, Florida, and California are commonly cited) and how each applies the requirement. -->

In consistency states, **the plan comes first**: if a parcel's zoning conflicts with the plan's designation, the usual fix is to rezone the parcel to match the plan, not to grant a variance or quietly ignore the conflict. And a planner reviewing a project that's inconsistent with the adopted plan must **report the inconsistency** in the staff analysis.

### Other kinds of plans

| Plan type | Scope | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| **Area (small area) plan** | A neighborhood, corridor, downtown, or station area | When citywide policy needs to be translated into **block-level direction** on land use, form, and access |
| **Functional plan** | One system citywide (a transportation master plan, parks plan, utility plan) | When a single topic needs deeper technical treatment |
| **Regional plan** | Multiple jurisdictions | For issues that cross boundaries: transportation, housing markets, watersheds |
| **Strategic plan** | An organization's priorities | For a limited set of near-term actions, usually three to five years |

An area plan that's **adopted as an amendment to the comprehensive plan** carries the plan's legal weight. One adopted merely **by resolution** as a guidance document generally **carries less weight** in a consistency review.

### Community character and form

Residents often care most about a place's "character," which is hard to regulate because it's hard to define. Good plans translate character into describable, measurable qualities: building placement and height, street width, tree canopy, the scale of storefronts, the relationship of buildings to sidewalks. Visual preference surveys, design workshops, and form-based approaches help (see [Growth management and innovative land use tools](/aicp/lessons/growth-management-and-innovative-tools)).

### Working with related disciplines

Comprehensive planning draws on engineering, landscape architecture, architecture, public health, law, economics, ecology, and public finance. Planners don't need to be experts in each, but must understand enough to ask good questions, integrate specialists' findings, and spot conflicts among them.

## Key terms

- **Comprehensive plan**: An adopted, long-range, general policy guide for a community's physical development.
- **Future land use map**: The plan's map of intended land use over the planning horizon.
- **Zoning map**: The official map of zoning districts in force now.
- **Element**: A topical chapter of a comprehensive plan.
- **Internal consistency**: Agreement among a plan's elements, maps, and policies.
- **Consistency doctrine**: The requirement in some states that zoning and approvals conform to the adopted plan.
- **Area (small area) plan**: A detailed plan for a neighborhood, corridor, district, or station area.
- **Functional plan**: A plan for a single system, such as transportation or parks.
- **Capital improvements element**: The plan element linking policies to facility investments and financing.
- **Community character**: The distinctive physical qualities that give a place its identity.

## Real-world examples

**Fixing a plan–zoning mismatch.** A parcel is designated low-density residential in the adopted plan but still zoned light industrial. An industrial user applies for a permit. In a consistency state, staff advise that the long-term fix is a city-initiated rezoning to match the plan. Staff also check with the city attorney about how the pending application must be handled under the zoning in effect when it was filed.

**A station area plan.** The comprehensive plan calls for "transit-supportive development near stations," but that's too general to guide a specific rezoning. The city prepares a station area plan with block-by-block height limits, required ground-floor uses on the main street, new pedestrian connections, and a parking strategy, and adopts it as an amendment to the comprehensive plan so it carries weight in future reviews.

**Adding a resilience element.** After repeated flooding, a coastal town adds a resilience element that maps projected flood risk, restricts new critical facilities in high-risk areas, and ties the capital program to elevating vulnerable roads.

## Exam tips

- A comprehensive plan is a **long-range policy guide**; zoning is the **regulation** that implements it.
- In **consistency** states, zoning and development approvals must conform to the plan. An inconsistent parcel is usually fixed by **rezoning to match the plan**.
- A small area plan translates citywide policy into **block-level** direction.
- An area plan adopted only by resolution generally carries **less weight** than one adopted into the comprehensive plan.
- The public facilities element coordinates most directly with the **capital improvements program**.

## Summary

A comprehensive plan is the adopted, long-range, general policy guide for physical development. It isn't a regulation; zoning implements it. Plans typically include land use, transportation, housing, economic development, environment, parks, facilities, hazards, and implementation elements, and those elements must be internally consistent. The plan's legal weight ranges from advisory to binding; in consistency states, zoning and approvals must conform to it. Use area plans to translate citywide policy into block-level direction, functional plans for single systems, regional plans for cross-boundary issues, and strategic plans for near-term organizational priorities.
