## Key concepts

### Who plans transportation

- **State departments of transportation (DOTs)** plan, build, and maintain state highways and distribute federal funds. Each state prepares a long-range statewide plan and a **Statewide Transportation Improvement Program (STIP)**.
- **Metropolitan planning organizations (MPOs)** are **federally required for every urbanized area with a population of at least 50,000**. They carry out the "continuing, comprehensive, and cooperative" (3C) regional planning process. An MPO's board typically includes local elected officials, the state DOT, and transit operators.
- **Transit agencies** plan and run bus and rail service.
- **Local governments** plan and manage local streets, sidewalks, parking, and land use.

### MPO plans and programs

| Product | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Metropolitan transportation plan** (long-range transportation plan) | The region's long-range plan, with a horizon of at least 20 years, **fiscally constrained** to reasonably expected revenues and updated periodically |
| **Transportation Improvement Program (TIP)** | The **short-range, fiscally constrained program** of projects to be funded over the next four or more years. **A project must be in the TIP before federal funds can be programmed for it.** |
| **Unified Planning Work Program (UPWP)** | The MPO's budget and work plan for its own planning activities |

In regions that don't meet federal air quality standards (**nonattainment** areas), the plan and TIP must also demonstrate **transportation conformity**: the projects must be consistent with the state's air quality plan and not worsen violations.

### Functional classification

Streets are classified by the role they play in the network:

- **Arterials** prioritize **mobility**: moving higher volumes over longer distances at higher speeds, with limited access.
- **Collectors** gather traffic from local streets and connect it to arterials, balancing mobility and access.
- **Local streets** prioritize **access** to adjacent property, at low speeds and volumes.

The core tradeoff is that the more a road serves through-movement, the less it should serve direct property access, and the reverse.

### The four-step travel demand model

The conventional regional travel model estimates future travel in four steps, **in this order**:

1. **Trip generation**: how many trips each zone produces and attracts, based on land use and demographics (households, jobs).
2. **Trip distribution**: where the trips go. It's often estimated with a **gravity model**, in which trips between zones increase with the zones' size and decrease with the distance or travel time between them.
3. **Mode choice (modal split)**: how the trips are made (car, transit, walking, biking).
4. **Trip assignment (route choice)**: which routes the trips take on the network.

Many large MPOs now also use **activity-based models**, which simulate individuals' daily schedules and tours of activity rather than isolated trips.

### Measuring performance: LOS and VMT

- **Level of service (LOS)** grades traffic operations from **A** (free flow) to **F** (breakdown). At **signalized intersections**, LOS is conventionally measured by **average control delay per vehicle**. LOS measures driver delay. Using it as a development standard tends to penalize infill and favor road widening.
- **Vehicle miles traveled (VMT)** measures the total amount of driving. As an impact metric, VMT **favors compact infill near transit** (which produces less driving per person) and penalizes dispersed development. **VMT per capita** is the best metric for evaluating a project's effect on regional greenhouse gas emissions. California's shift from LOS to VMT in environmental review is the best-known example. <!-- VERIFY: California's SB 743 (2013) directed the shift from LOS to VMT for CEQA transportation analysis; confirm details if expanded. -->

### Induced demand

Adding road capacity lowers the "price" of driving (in time), which **generates additional trips over time**: people drive more, take new routes, travel at peak times, or move farther out. That's **induced demand**. Widening a congested road rarely solves congestion permanently. It's the best professional answer to "Will widening fix this for good?"

### Complete streets and safety

- A **complete street** is designed to **safely accommodate users of all ages and abilities**, whether walking, biking, using transit, driving, or delivering goods. Complete streets policies commit agencies to consider all users in every project.
- A **road diet** most commonly converts a **four-lane undivided road to two through lanes with a center turn lane**, often adding bike lanes. It typically reduces crashes with little effect on capacity at moderate volumes.
- **Traffic calming** uses physical design (narrower lanes, speed humps, curb extensions, roundabouts) to slow vehicles.
- **Vision Zero** is a framework **aimed at eliminating traffic deaths and serious injuries**. It treats them as preventable, and relies on the **safe system approach**: humans make mistakes, so the system (street design, speeds, vehicles) must be forgiving of them.

:::checkpoint cp:trans-safe-system

### Transit-oriented development

**Transit-oriented development (TOD)** concentrates housing, jobs, and services within walking distance of transit stations, generally **a quarter to a half mile**. Its characteristics:

- compact, moderate-to-high density near the station;
- a mix of uses within walking distance;
- a safe, connected, walkable street network; and
- **reduced or eliminated parking minimums**, with parking managed rather than maximized.

Station-area planning should also address **displacement risk**, because new transit can raise land values quickly. Pair TOD plans with preservation, inclusionary requirements, and tenant protections. Adopting a station area plan and rezoning in advance helps capture development potential before speculation raises land costs.

:::checkpoint cp:trans-tod-displacement

### Travel demand management

**Transportation (travel) demand management (TDM)** reduces or shifts driving demand rather than adding capacity: **carpool and vanpool incentives, transit passes**, parking pricing and parking cash-out, telework, flexible schedules, and bike facilities.

### Emerging issues

**Automated vehicles** and app-based services are most likely to challenge conventional assumptions about **parking and curb management**: pick-up and drop-off space, delivery zones, and how much parking new buildings need. Micromobility (scooters, e-bikes) raises questions about street space and safety.

## Key terms

- **MPO**: The federally required regional transportation planning body for urbanized areas of 50,000 or more people.
- **Transportation Improvement Program (TIP)**: The MPO's short-range, fiscally constrained program of funded projects.
- **Fiscal constraint**: The requirement that plans and programs fit within reasonably expected revenue.
- **Transportation conformity**: The demonstration that transportation plans are consistent with air quality plans.
- **Functional classification**: Grouping streets as arterials, collectors, and locals by function.
- **Four-step model**: Trip generation, trip distribution, mode choice, and trip assignment.
- **Gravity model**: A trip distribution method based on zone size and separation.
- **Level of service (LOS)**: A letter grade of traffic operations, from A to F.
- **Vehicle miles traveled (VMT)**: Total miles driven by vehicles in an area and period.
- **Induced demand**: Additional driving generated by added road capacity.
- **Complete street**: A street designed for safe use by people of all ages and abilities in all modes.
- **Road diet**: Reallocating lanes, typically from four lanes to two plus a center turn lane.
- **Vision Zero**: A strategy to eliminate traffic deaths and serious injuries.
- **Transit-oriented development (TOD)**: Compact, mixed-use development within walking distance of transit.
- **Travel demand management (TDM)**: Strategies that reduce or shift travel demand.

## Real-world examples

**A road diet.** A four-lane street through a neighborhood business district carries moderate traffic and has a history of rear-end and pedestrian crashes. The city restripes it to one lane each way with a center turn lane and bike lanes. After a year, crashes fall, travel times barely change, and businesses report more foot traffic.

**Getting a project funded.** A city wants federal money for a bus rapid transit line. It works with the MPO to add the project to the long-range plan and then the TIP, because the region is in nonattainment, runs a conformity analysis, and completes environmental review.

**Measuring impacts differently.** A city switches its development impact metric from intersection LOS to VMT per capita. A 300-unit infill project next to a rail station, which previously required costly intersection widening to avoid "LOS F," now shows low VMT and needs no road widening. A proposed exurban subdivision now faces TDM requirements.

## Exam tips

- The four-step order is **generation → distribution → mode choice → assignment**.
- MPOs are required for urbanized areas of **50,000+**. Projects must be in the **TIP** to get federal funds.
- Signalized intersection LOS is measured by **average control delay per vehicle**.
- "Will widening fix congestion permanently?" is answered by **induced demand**.
- For a transit project's GHG effect, use **VMT per capita**, not LOS.
- TOD is typically within **a quarter to a half mile** of a station, with reduced parking.

## Summary

MPOs, required for urbanized areas of 50,000 or more, produce long-range plans and TIPs; projects must be in the TIP to receive federal funds, and nonattainment regions must show conformity. Arterials favor mobility and local streets favor access. The four-step model runs from trip generation to distribution (gravity model) to mode choice to assignment. LOS measures driver delay; VMT measures driving, favors infill, and is the right metric for emissions. Added capacity induces demand. Complete streets, road diets, and Vision Zero's safe system approach improve safety. TOD concentrates growth within a quarter to half mile of stations, with reduced parking and anti-displacement measures, and TDM reduces driving demand.
