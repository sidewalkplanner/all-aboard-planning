## Learning objectives

- Evaluate plan alternatives against goals using a transparent, weighted method.
- Explain when scenario planning is the right tool and how a scenario process works.
- Describe a buildable lands (land capacity) inventory and how it's compared with projected demand.
- Compare fiscal impact analysis methods, including the per capita multiplier.
- Describe how a local greenhouse gas inventory is built, and how pilots and best practices can be tested before full adoption.

## Key concepts

### Comparing alternatives

Once a planning process has two or more alternatives, it needs a fair way to compare them. Good evaluation is:

- **tied to the plan's goals**: each goal becomes one or more evaluation criteria;
- **measurable where possible**: acres of farmland consumed, households within a 10-minute walk of transit, infrastructure cost, vehicle miles traveled;
- **transparent about weighting**: if housing affordability counts twice as much as traffic delay, say so; and
- **honest about uncertainty.**

A **goals-achievement matrix** lays out alternatives in columns and criteria in rows, scoring how well each alternative advances each goal, sometimes with weights. **Multi-criteria analysis** is the broader family of methods for this. **Sensitivity analysis** tests whether the preferred alternative changes when weights or assumptions change. If a small change in weights flips the result, decision-makers should know that.

:::checkpoint cp:analysis-sensitivity

### Scenario planning

**Scenario planning** develops several plausible, internally consistent pictures of the future and uses them to test strategies. It's most useful when a community faces **deep uncertainty about conditions largely outside its control**: shifts in the economy, climate impacts, technology such as automated vehicles, or demographic change.

A typical process:

1. Identify the **driving forces** and key uncertainties.
2. Build three or four distinct scenarios, such as "rapid growth, high climate impact" and "slow growth, remote work."
3. Model the implications of each (land consumption, travel, infrastructure, fiscal effects).
4. Engage the public on the tradeoffs, often with interactive tools.
5. Identify **robust strategies** that perform acceptably across all scenarios, and **signposts** to watch that show which future is unfolding.

Scenarios are **not predictions**. Some processes use *exploratory* scenarios (what could happen?) and others *normative* ones (what do we want to happen, and how do we get there?).

### Buildable lands and capacity analysis

Before a plan can say where growth goes, it needs to know how much land can take it. A **buildable lands inventory** (or land capacity analysis):

1. starts with **vacant** land and **underused** (redevelopable) land;
2. removes **constrained** land: floodplains, wetlands, steep slopes, protected habitat, and land committed to public uses;
3. applies the density or intensity allowed (or proposed), often discounted for realistic market absorption; and
4. compares the resulting **capacity** with **projected demand** for housing and jobs.

If demand exceeds capacity, and the jurisdiction can't or won't expand outward (because of an urban growth boundary, for example), the plan must accommodate growth through **infill, redevelopment, and increased intensity** on underused land.

:::checkpoint cp:analysis-buildable

### Fiscal impact analysis

A **fiscal impact analysis** estimates the **public costs and public revenues** a proposed development, annexation, or land use pattern will generate for local governments and school districts. Its question is *net* fiscal impact.

Common methods:

| Method | How it estimates costs |
|---|---|
| **Per capita multiplier** | Multiplies new residents (and sometimes employees) by the jurisdiction's **average current cost per person** for each service. It's simple and the most widely used, but it assumes average costs apply to new growth. |
| **Case study** | Interviews department heads about the specific added costs of this project, such as a new patrol car or another fire engine company. |
| **Service standard** | Applies adopted service standards (officers per 1,000 residents, for example) to the new population. |
| **Comparable city** | Uses cost patterns in similar communities. |
| **Marginal cost** | Estimates the cost of serving *additional* growth, considering excess or deficient capacity. It's more accurate but data-intensive. |

Fiscal impact is one input, not the answer. A development can be fiscally negative and still be the right choice. Affordable housing and parks are examples. The analysis simply makes the tradeoff visible. Development patterns matter too: compact development generally costs less to serve per household than dispersed development, because pipes, roads, and service routes are shorter.

### Greenhouse gas inventories

A local **greenhouse gas (GHG) inventory** is the baseline for a climate action plan. It usually includes:

- **community-wide emissions** by sector (transportation, residential and commercial energy, industry, waste, and sometimes agriculture); and
- **government operations** emissions (the city's own buildings, fleet, and facilities).

Emissions are often grouped by **scope**: Scope 1 covers direct emissions, Scope 2 covers purchased electricity, and Scope 3 covers other indirect emissions. Once you have the baseline, you set reduction targets, model strategies, and re-inventory periodically to track progress.

### Innovations and best practices

Planners are expected to apply innovations responsibly:

- **Look for evidence**: what happened where the idea was tried, and how similar is that place?
- **Pilot first**: temporary or small-scale trials (tactical urbanism, pilot parking programs, demonstration projects) test an idea before committing capital or rewriting codes.
- **Evaluate and adjust**: set metrics before the pilot starts.
- **Share results** with peers and the public.

### Identifying consequences

Every alternative has second-order effects. Good analysis traces them: a parking reduction may shift demand onto neighborhood streets; a new park may raise nearby rents; an industrial rezoning may displace small businesses. Naming those possible consequences, and the ways to mitigate them, is part of the analysis, not an afterthought.

## Key terms

- **Goals-achievement matrix**: A table scoring alternatives against plan goals, often with weights.
- **Multi-criteria analysis**: Methods for comparing alternatives across several criteria.
- **Sensitivity analysis**: Testing how results change when assumptions or weights change.
- **Scenario planning**: Developing several plausible futures to test the robustness of strategies.
- **Robust strategy**: A strategy that performs acceptably across multiple scenarios.
- **Buildable lands inventory**: An estimate of land available and suitable for development, and its capacity.
- **Redevelopable land**: Developed land whose improvements are worth little relative to the land, making redevelopment likely.
- **Fiscal impact analysis**: An estimate of the public costs and revenues of development.
- **Per capita multiplier method**: A fiscal impact method using average cost per resident.
- **Marginal cost**: The added cost of serving additional development.
- **Greenhouse gas inventory**: An accounting of emissions by sector and source, used as a baseline.
- **Pilot project**: A temporary or small-scale trial used to test an idea.

## Real-world examples

**Regional scenario planning.** A fast-growing region builds four growth scenarios for 2050, from "business as usual" dispersed growth to "centers and corridors." Residents use an online tool to compare land consumption, commute times, water use, and infrastructure costs across the scenarios. The adopted regional vision favors compact centers, and local plans later adopt policies consistent with it.

**A buildable lands gap.** A city inside an urban growth boundary projects demand for 12,000 new homes over 20 years. The buildable lands inventory shows capacity for only 7,500 under current zoning, after removing floodplains and steep slopes. The plan closes the gap by upzoning commercial corridors and allowing middle housing in residential districts, rather than proposing a boundary expansion.

**A fiscal impact review.** A developer proposes annexing 400 acres for single-family homes. A per capita analysis suggests property taxes will cover costs, but a case-study review reveals the project would need a new fire station and a sewer lift station. The city negotiates for the developer to fund the lift station and phase the project with the station's opening.

## Exam tips

- Scenario planning fits **deep uncertainty about conditions outside local control**.
- Fiscal impact analysis estimates **net public costs and revenues**. The **per capita multiplier** method uses average cost per resident.
- To test whether land can hold projected growth, prepare a **buildable lands inventory**.
- If growth exceeds capacity inside a fixed boundary, the answer is **infill, redevelopment, and higher intensity**.
- A GHG inventory covers **community-wide and government-operations** emissions by sector.

## Summary

Evaluate alternatives transparently against the plan's goals, with explicit weights and sensitivity tests. Use scenario planning when the future is deeply uncertain and largely outside local control, and look for robust strategies that work across scenarios. A buildable lands inventory compares realistic land capacity with projected demand, and a gap inside a fixed boundary points to infill and intensification. Fiscal impact analysis estimates net public costs and revenues; the per capita multiplier method is the most common and the simplest. GHG inventories set climate baselines by sector. Pilot innovations before scaling them, and trace consequences before choosing.
