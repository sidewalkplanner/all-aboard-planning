## Learning objectives

- Explain what subdivision regulations govern and how they differ from zoning.
- Put the steps of subdivision approval in order, from sketch plan to recorded final plat.
- Describe improvement guarantees such as performance bonds, and the role of dedications.
- Explain site plan review and how staff assess a proposal for consistency with the plan and code.
- Explain vested rights, development agreements, negotiated exactions, and the official map.

## Key concepts

### What subdivision regulations do

**Zoning** controls what can be built on a lot and how big it can be. **Subdivision regulations** control **how land is divided into lots** and the **infrastructure that serves them**: streets, sidewalks, water and sewer lines, stormwater systems, and sometimes parks and school sites. Authority for subdivision control usually comes from state enabling legislation, and many states' laws trace back to the 1928 Standard City Planning Enabling Act.

Subdivision rules protect future residents and the public: they make sure lots are buildable and legally described, that streets connect and meet standards, and that the developer, not taxpayers, pays for the local improvements the development needs.

### The approval process

Terms vary, but the typical sequence is:

1. **Pre-application meeting or sketch plan.** Staff and the applicant discuss the concept, the regulations, and issues early, before expensive engineering.
2. **Preliminary plat.** This is the main design review: lot layout, street pattern, utilities, grading, drainage, and dedications. It's often approved by the planning commission, sometimes with conditions.
3. **Construction plans and improvements.** The developer either **builds the required public improvements** or **guarantees** they'll be built.
4. **Final plat.** This is the precise, surveyed map that **legally creates the lots**. Once approved, it's **recorded** with the county recorder, and lots can then be sold.
5. **Acceptance and maintenance.** The local government inspects and formally accepts dedicated improvements, often requiring a maintenance guarantee for a set period.

Many codes offer a simpler **minor subdivision** or lot-split process for divisions of just a few lots that need no new streets.

### Improvement guarantees and dedications

- A **performance guarantee**, such as a **performance bond**, letter of credit, or cash escrow, **guarantees completion of the required public improvements**. If the developer defaults, the government can draw on it to finish the work.
- A **maintenance bond** covers defects for a period after the improvements are accepted.
- **Dedications** transfer land or improvements, such as street right-of-way or utility easements, to public ownership. Park land may be dedicated, or a **fee in lieu** paid instead. Dedications and fees are **exactions** and must meet the nexus and proportionality tests (see [Takings, exactions, and property rights](/aicp/lessons/takings-and-exactions)).

### Site plan review

**Site plan review** examines the detailed layout of a single development project: building placement, access and circulation, parking, loading, landscaping, lighting, stormwater, and utilities. It checks compliance with zoning and design standards. Depending on the code, it's administrative (by staff), or decided by the planning commission for larger projects.

Many jurisdictions route applications through an interdepartmental **development review committee** (planning, engineering, fire, utilities, parks, and transportation) so the applicant gets one coordinated set of comments.

### Consistency review

For discretionary applications, staff assess whether the proposal is consistent with:

- the **comprehensive plan** and any area plan;
- the **zoning and subdivision standards**; and
- other adopted policies, such as the transportation plan and design guidelines.

When a proposal is **inconsistent with the adopted plan**, the planner's professional obligation is to **report the inconsistency clearly in the staff analysis**, even if the project is popular. Decision-makers may still approve an amendment, but they should do so knowingly.

### Vested rights

**Vested rights** protect a developer's right to proceed under the rules in place at a certain point, even if the rules later change. Under the **common law rule** followed in most states, rights vest when the developer has **made substantial expenditures (or incurred substantial obligations) in good-faith reliance on a validly issued permit**, usually a building permit. Some states vest earlier by statute, for example at the filing of a complete application or at approval of a preliminary plat. <!-- VERIFY: vesting rules vary widely by state; if specific states are named, confirm them. -->

### Development agreements

A **development agreement** is a contract between a local government and a developer, authorized by state statute in many states, that typically:

- **locks in the development standards** that will apply for a defined period, giving the developer **vested rights** and certainty for a large, multi-phase project; and
- secures **public benefits** in return, such as infrastructure, affordable housing, parks, or phasing commitments.

Development agreements are adopted through a public process and must stay within the jurisdiction's statutory authority.

### Negotiated exactions

When a developer offers improvements beyond what the code strictly requires, such as a park, extra right-of-way, or a traffic signal, as part of a rezoning or development agreement, these are **negotiated exactions**. Courts still expect them to meet the *Nollan*/*Dolan* standards of essential nexus and rough proportionality when the government demands them as a condition of approval.

### The official map

An **official map** is an adopted map that **reserves the locations of future streets, parks, and other public facilities**. It shows property owners and developers where public improvements are planned, so they aren't built over. Because it can restrict private land for long periods, many states limit how long a reservation lasts before the government must acquire the land or release it.

## Key terms

- **Subdivision regulations**: Rules governing the division of land into lots and the required infrastructure.
- **Sketch plan**: An early concept plan discussed before formal application.
- **Preliminary plat**: The detailed proposed layout of a subdivision reviewed for design and engineering.
- **Final plat**: The surveyed, recorded map that legally creates the lots.
- **Performance bond**: A guarantee that required public improvements will be completed.
- **Dedication**: The transfer of land or improvements to public ownership as a condition of approval.
- **Fee in lieu**: A payment made instead of dedicating land or building an improvement.
- **Site plan review**: Review of a single project's detailed layout for code compliance.
- **Vested rights**: The right to proceed under existing rules despite later changes.
- **Development agreement**: A contract fixing development standards for a period in exchange for public benefits.
- **Negotiated exaction**: An improvement or dedication agreed to in negotiation, beyond code minimums.
- **Official map**: An adopted map reserving locations for future public facilities.

## Real-world examples

**A 120-lot subdivision.** A developer brings a sketch plan to a pre-application meeting. Staff point out that the plan dead-ends two streets that the transportation plan shows connecting to a future school. The preliminary plat is revised to connect them. The developer posts a performance bond covering the streets, water, sewer, and stormwater ponds, builds them, and the final plat is recorded. After a two-year maintenance period, the city accepts the streets.

**A multi-phase development agreement.** A 300-acre mixed-use project will build out over 15 years. The city and developer sign a development agreement that locks in the zoning standards for 15 years. In return, the developer commits to building a fire station site, a trail network, and 10% affordable housing in each phase.

**Reserving a future road.** A county's transportation plan calls for a new arterial through farmland that's likely to develop soon. The county adopts an official map showing the alignment, so new subdivisions reserve the right-of-way in their layouts rather than building homes where the road will go.

## Exam tips

- The **final plat** legally creates the lots and is recorded; the preliminary plat is the main design review.
- A **performance bond** guarantees required public improvements will be completed.
- Common-law vested rights: **substantial expenditures in good faith under a valid permit**.
- A **development agreement** trades locked-in standards (vested rights) for public benefits.
- An **official map** reserves locations for future streets and facilities.
- When a project is inconsistent with the plan, the staff analysis must say so.

## Summary

Subdivision regulations govern how land is divided and the infrastructure that serves it; zoning governs what's built on the lots. Approval runs from sketch plan to preliminary plat, improvements or a performance guarantee, and a recorded final plat that legally creates the lots. Dedications and fees in lieu are exactions subject to nexus and proportionality. Site plan review checks a single project's layout, and consistency review compares proposals with the plan and code; report inconsistencies honestly. Vested rights generally arise from substantial expenditures under a valid permit. Development agreements trade certainty for public benefits, and the official map reserves land for future public facilities.
