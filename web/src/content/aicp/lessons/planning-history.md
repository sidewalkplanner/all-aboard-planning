## Learning objectives

- Place the major American planning movements in chronological order and explain what problem each was responding to.
- Connect key figures (Olmsted, Burnham, Howard, Perry, Stein and Wright, Mumford, Moses, Jacobs) to their ideas and projects.
- Explain how federal programs from the New Deal through urban renewal and the Interstate era reshaped cities.
- Describe the reactions that followed: preservation, advocacy planning, environmental planning, New Urbanism, and Smart Growth.
- Trace how the planning profession organized itself, up to the founding of APA and AICP.

## Key concepts

The exam doesn't expect you to memorize every date. It expects you to know **what came first, what each movement was reacting against, and who is associated with what.** Think of planning history as a chain of problems and responses.

### 1. Crowding, disease, and reform (mid-1800s to early 1900s)

Rapid industrialization packed immigrants and workers into dense, unsanitary tenements. Epidemics made public health the first great driver of city planning.

- **Sanitary reform** brought water systems, sewers, and street cleaning.
- **Tenement reform** set minimum standards for light, air, and sanitation. Jacob Riis's photographs in *How the Other Half Lives* (1890) built public pressure. New York's **Tenement House Act of 1901**, associated with reformer Lawrence Veiller, required better light, ventilation, and sanitation in new tenements.
- **Parks** were seen as the "lungs of the city." **Frederick Law Olmsted** and **Calvert Vaux** designed Central Park, and later **Riverside, Illinois (1869)**, one of the earliest planned suburbs in the U.S., with curving streets that followed the land.

### 2. The City Beautiful (1890s to 1910s)

The **1893 World's Columbian Exposition** in Chicago, with its "White City" of classical buildings planned under **Daniel Burnham**, launched the **City Beautiful movement**. Its belief was that monumental civic design, grand boulevards, and unified public buildings would inspire civic virtue and order.

- The **McMillan Plan** (1901–1902) revived and extended L'Enfant's plan for Washington, D.C., including the National Mall.
- The **1909 Plan of Chicago** by Burnham and Edward Bennett is the movement's landmark: a regional vision of boulevards, lakefront parks, rail consolidation, and civic centers. Its ambition is often summed up by "make no little plans," a line popularly attributed to Burnham. <!-- VERIFY: the "make no little plans" quotation's attribution to Burnham is disputed; keep the "popularly attributed" hedge or remove. -->

Critics said City Beautiful focused on appearances and civic monuments while neglecting housing and social conditions. The next wave, sometimes called the "City Practical" or "City Efficient," emphasized engineering, zoning, and administration.

### 3. The profession and zoning take shape (1900s to 1920s)

- The **first National Conference on City Planning** met in 1909.
- Cities began creating official planning commissions; Hartford, Connecticut, is often cited as the first, in 1907. <!-- VERIFY: Hartford 1907 as the first official municipal planning commission is commonly cited; confirm before publishing. -->
- **New York City's 1916 Zoning Resolution**, the first comprehensive zoning ordinance in the U.S., responded to tall buildings (the Equitable Building is the usual example) blocking light and air, and to manufacturing encroaching on the Fifth Avenue retail district. Its height-and-setback rules produced the classic "wedding cake" skyscraper.
- The U.S. Department of Commerce, under Herbert Hoover, published model enabling laws: the **Standard State Zoning Enabling Act** (first circulated in 1922, with revised editions through 1926) <!-- VERIFY: SZEA publication history is given variously as 1922, 1924, and 1926; confirm the dates you want to teach. --> and the **Standard City Planning Enabling Act** (1928).
- The Supreme Court upheld zoning in ***Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co.*** **(1926)**. See [Foundations of land use law](/aicp/lessons/land-use-law-foundations).
- In 1925, **Cincinnati** became the first major U.S. city to officially adopt a comprehensive plan.

### 4. The Garden City and the regional planners (1898 to 1930s)

**Ebenezer Howard**'s *To-Morrow: A Peaceful Path to Real Reform* (1898), reissued in 1902 as ***Garden Cities of To-Morrow***, proposed self-contained towns of limited size, about 32,000 people, surrounded by a permanent **greenbelt** of farmland, with land held in common so rising land values would benefit the community. When a town reached its limit, growth would go to a new garden city, forming a linked cluster Howard called the "social city." Letchworth and Welwyn Garden City in England were built on these ideas.

In the U.S., the ideas spread through:

- **Clarence Perry's neighborhood unit** (published in the 1929 *Regional Plan of New York and Its Environs*): a residential area sized to support an **elementary school** at its center, with arterial streets at the edges rather than running through it, local shops at the corners, and small parks throughout.
- **Radburn, New Jersey (1929)**, designed by **Clarence Stein and Henry Wright**: "a town for the motor age," with **superblocks**, **cul-de-sacs**, homes facing interior parks, and pedestrian paths separated from car traffic by underpasses.
- The **Regional Planning Association of America** (founded in 1923), including **Lewis Mumford**, Stein, Wright, and Benton MacKaye (who proposed the Appalachian Trail). It argued for planning at the scale of whole regions and ecosystems.

### 5. The New Deal (1930s)

The Depression brought the federal government into planning.

- The **Resettlement Administration**, led by Rexford Tugwell, built three **greenbelt towns**: Greenbelt, Maryland; Greenhills, Ohio; and Greendale, Wisconsin.
- The **Tennessee Valley Authority** (1933) was an experiment in regional planning for an entire river basin: flood control, power, and economic development.
- The **Home Owners' Loan Corporation** and later the Federal Housing Administration shaped mortgage lending. Their neighborhood risk maps are associated with **redlining**. See [Patterns of human settlement](/aicp/lessons/urban-form-and-settlement).
- The **Housing Act of 1937** created the federal public housing program.

### 6. Urban renewal and the highway era (1949 to 1960s)

- The **Housing Act of 1949** launched **urban renewal** (Title I): federal money for cities to acquire and clear "blighted" areas for redevelopment. The **Housing Act of 1954** broadened it and created the **Section 701** planning grant program, which paid for local and regional comprehensive plans and helped staff many new planning offices.
- The **Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956** funded the Interstate system. Urban freeways sped suburbanization and cut through, and often destroyed, many Black and immigrant neighborhoods.
- **Robert Moses**, who held many New York City and state offices at once, built parks, bridges, highways, and housing on a vast scale, often at great cost to the neighborhoods in his way.

### 7. The backlash (1960s to 1970s)

- **Jane Jacobs**'s ***The Death and Life of Great American Cities*** (1961) attacked urban renewal and modernist planning. She argued that lively, safe streets depend on a fine-grained mix of uses, short blocks, buildings of varying age, and enough density, and that "eyes on the street" keep neighborhoods safe. She opposed superblocks and towers set in open space.
- The demolition of New York's Pennsylvania Station in the 1960s galvanized the historic preservation movement, and the **National Historic Preservation Act** passed in 1966.
- **Paul Davidoff**'s "Advocacy and Pluralism in Planning" (1965) and **Sherry Arnstein**'s "A Ladder of Citizen Participation" (1969) challenged top-down planning. See [Planning theory](/aicp/lessons/planning-theory).
- McHarg's *Design with Nature* (1969) and the **National Environmental Policy Act** (signed January 1, 1970) brought environmental analysis into mainstream planning.
- Some states began reclaiming land use authority from localities, a shift sometimes called the "quiet revolution" in land use control. Oregon's statewide planning program, with its urban growth boundaries, dates from 1973. <!-- VERIFY: Oregon's statewide land use planning law (Senate Bill 100) is commonly dated to 1973; confirm. -->

### 8. The profession consolidates (1978)

The **American Institute of Planners** (founded in 1917 as the American City Planning Institute) and the **American Society of Planning Officials** (founded in 1934) merged in **1978** to form the **American Planning Association**. The **American Institute of Certified Planners** was created at the same time as APA's professional institute. <!-- VERIFY: confirm the founding years 1917 (ACPI/AIP) and 1934 (ASPO). -->

### 9. New Urbanism, Smart Growth, and sustainability (1980s to today)

- **New Urbanism** revived traditional town design: walkable, mixed-use neighborhoods with a range of housing types, a connected street grid, and a strong public realm. **Seaside, Florida**, designed by Andrés Duany and Elizabeth Plater-Zyberk in the early 1980s, is the famous early built example. The **Congress for the New Urbanism** was founded in 1993. <!-- VERIFY: confirm CNU's founding year (1993). -->
- **Smart Growth**, from the 1990s, focused on regional growth policy: directing development to existing communities and infrastructure, protecting open space and farmland, and offering transportation choices. Maryland's Smart Growth program (1997) is an often-cited state example. <!-- VERIFY: confirm Maryland Smart Growth program year (1997). -->
- The 1987 Brundtland Commission report, *Our Common Future*, popularized **sustainable development**: meeting present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet theirs. Planners often frame sustainability as the "three E's": **environment, economy, and equity**.

## Key terms

- **Sanitary reform**: Nineteenth-century public health improvements such as water and sewer systems.
- **City Beautiful movement**: A late-1800s to early-1900s movement for monumental civic design, launched by the 1893 Chicago exposition.
- **Plan of Chicago (1909)**: Burnham and Bennett's regional plan, the landmark of the City Beautiful era.
- **Garden City**: Howard's concept of a size-limited, self-contained town ringed by a permanent greenbelt.
- **Neighborhood unit**: Perry's residential unit centered on an elementary school, bounded by arterials.
- **Radburn**: Stein and Wright's 1929 New Jersey town of superblocks and cul-de-sacs, with separated pedestrian paths.
- **Greenbelt towns**: Three New Deal communities built by the Resettlement Administration.
- **Urban renewal**: The federal program, launched in 1949, that funded clearing and redeveloping "blighted" areas.
- **Section 701**: The 1954 Housing Act program that funded local and regional planning.
- **New Urbanism**: A design movement for walkable, mixed-use, traditional neighborhoods.
- **Smart Growth**: Regional growth policy favoring compact development, infill, and transportation choice.
- **Three E's**: Environment, economy, and equity, the pillars of sustainability.

## Real-world examples

**Reading a city's layers.** Walk through almost any older American city and you can see this history. There's a downtown grid, a City Beautiful civic center with a courthouse and library around a formal plaza, a 1920s streetcar suburb, a postwar subdivision of cul-de-sacs, an Interstate that split a historic Black neighborhood, a 1970s historic district, and a 2000s mixed-use infill project near a new rail station. Planners working on any of those places inherit the decisions, and the harms, of earlier eras.

**Repairing a highway's damage.** Several cities are now studying or pursuing projects to cap, remove, or reconnect neighborhoods divided by urban freeways built in the 1950s and 1960s. Understanding why those highways were routed where they were helps planners engage affected communities honestly.

## Exam tips

- Know the order: **City Beautiful (1890s) → Garden City ideas in the U.S. (1900s–1920s) → New Deal (1930s) → urban renewal and Interstates (1949–1960s) → backlash (1960s–70s) → New Urbanism (1980s) → Smart Growth (1990s)**. Garden City and City Beautiful overlap, but City Beautiful's American peak came first.
- Pair people with ideas: Burnham–Chicago, Howard–Garden City, Perry–neighborhood unit, Stein and Wright–Radburn, Jacobs–*Death and Life*, Moses–large-scale public works, McHarg–overlay method.

## Summary

American planning grew out of responses to crowding and disease, then passed through the monumental City Beautiful, the Garden City and regional planning ideas, and the New Deal's federal experiments. Urban renewal and the Interstate program transformed cities after World War II, often destructively, and provoked a backlash that produced historic preservation, advocacy planning, and environmental review. The profession consolidated into APA and AICP in 1978. New Urbanism and Smart Growth, framed by sustainability's three E's, shaped planning from the 1980s on.
