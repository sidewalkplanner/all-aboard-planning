## Learning objectives

- Describe how the AICP Code is organized, and explain the difference between aspirational principles and enforceable Rules of Conduct.
- Summarize the three groups of aspirational principles: responsibility to the public, to clients and employers, and to the profession and colleagues.
- Explain how members get ethics advice: informal advice and formal advisory opinions.
- Describe how a charge of misconduct is filed, investigated, and resolved, and the sanctions available.
- Recognize which part of the Code an exam question is really asking about.

## Key concepts

### Who the Code applies to

The Code applies to **AICP members**, meaning certified planners, and in some provisions to candidates in AICP's programs. APA has a separate, broader ethical statement for all members. On the exam, "the Code" means the AICP Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct. <!-- VERIFY: confirm to whom the Code currently applies (AICP members, and whether candidates are covered). -->

### Structure: principles and rules

The single most tested idea about the Code is its two-part structure:

| Part | Nature | Enforceable? |
|---|---|---|
| **Aspirational principles** ("principles to which we aspire") | Ideals that describe the kind of professional conduct planners strive for | **No.** A failure to meet an aspirational principle can't by itself support a charge of misconduct. |
| **Rules of Conduct** | Specific standards of required and prohibited behavior | **Yes.** Violations can lead to sanctions. |

The Code also contains **procedures** for advisory opinions, charges, investigations, and sanctions.

So the commitment to seek social justice by expanding choice and opportunity for everyone is an **aspirational principle**, not a rule, while the prohibition on accepting gifts that could reasonably appear to influence your judgment is a **Rule of Conduct**.

:::figure fig-code-parts | Three parts of the Code, with whether each is enforceable. Aspirational principles, ideals to strive for: no. Rules of Conduct, required and prohibited acts: yes. Procedures, covering advice, charges, and sanctions: how the Code runs. Note: only a rule can be violated.
When a question asks whether conduct violates the Code, look for a Rule of Conduct; falling short of a principle isn't a violation.
:::

### The aspirational principles

The principles are grouped by the three relationships a planner has. In summary:

**1. Overall responsibility to the public.** This is the first and most important of the three. Planners aspire to:

- serve the **public interest**, recognizing the rights of others and the long-range consequences of present actions;
- **consider the long-range consequences** of present actions and the **interrelatedness** of decisions;
- provide **full, clear, accurate, and timely information** on planning issues to everyone affected, and give people a genuine opportunity to influence plans that affect them, including those who lack formal organization or influence;
- **seek social justice**: expand choice and opportunity for all, recognize a special responsibility to the needs of disadvantaged groups, and work to change policies and decisions that oppose these needs;
- promote **racial and economic equity** and work to eliminate historic patterns of inequity; <!-- VERIFY: confirm that the current Code's principles include explicit language on racial and economic equity and on eliminating historic inequities. -->
- protect the integrity and heritage of the **natural and built environment**; and
- deal **fairly with all participants** in the planning process.

**2. Responsibility to clients and employers.** Planners aspire to:

- exercise **independent professional judgment** on behalf of clients and employers;
- accept a client's or employer's decisions about the objectives and nature of the services, **unless** the course of action is illegal or plainly inconsistent with the planner's primary obligation to the public interest; and
- avoid conflicts of interest in their professional work.

**3. Responsibility to the profession and colleagues.** Planners aspire to:

- protect and enhance the **integrity of the profession** and be accountable for their conduct;
- **educate** the public about planning and help colleagues and students grow through **mentoring** and sharing knowledge;
- treat colleagues fairly and fairly represent others' views; and
- contribute time and resources to professional activities and continue their own professional development.

### The Rules of Conduct (overview)

The Rules of Conduct turn the most important obligations into enforceable standards. They're covered in detail in [Conflicts of interest and the Rules of Conduct](/aicp/lessons/conflicts-of-interest-and-rules-of-conduct). Their main themes are:

- **Honesty**: don't knowingly misrepresent facts, findings, or qualifications.
- **Conflicts of interest**: disclose them and step back from matters where you have a personal or financial interest.
- **Gifts and advantage**: don't accept gifts or favors that could reasonably appear to influence you, and don't use your position for personal advantage.
- **Confidentiality**: don't disclose information received in confidence, except as the law requires.
- **Competence**: accept only work you're competent to do, or do it under the supervision of someone who is.
- **Advocacy**: when advocating publicly, identify whom you represent.
- **Process integrity**: don't file frivolous charges, retaliate against people who raise ethics concerns, or obstruct an investigation.

### Getting advice

A member who's **unsure whether a contemplated course of action complies with the Code** has two routes:

- **Informal advice** from AICP's **Ethics Officer**, which is quick and not binding.
- A **formal advisory opinion**, which the member can request in writing from the Ethics Officer. Formal opinions may be published, without identifying the member, to guide others.

Asking for advice *before* acting is the appropriate professional step when a situation is genuinely uncertain. <!-- VERIFY: confirm the current names and process for informal advice and formal advisory opinions under the Code's procedures. -->

### Charges, investigation, and sanctions

- **Who can file:** **any person**, whether or not they're a planner or APA member, may file a charge of misconduct alleging that an AICP member violated the Rules of Conduct.
- **Process:** the Ethics Officer reviews the charge, may dismiss it or investigate, and the member has an opportunity to respond. Matters that aren't dismissed or settled go to AICP's **Ethics Committee** for decision, with a right of appeal.
- **Sanctions** for a proven violation include a **reprimand, suspension, or expulsion** from AICP (sanctions may be published). AICP can't impose criminal penalties such as fines or imprisonment; those come only from the legal system.
- Filing a charge **known to be frivolous** is itself a violation.

### Reading exam questions about the Code

When you see an ethics question, first ask **which part of the Code it's testing**:

- **Structure questions** ("Which is enforceable?"): only the Rules of Conduct are.
- **Principle questions** ("The Code's commitment to social justice appears where?"): the aspirational principles.
- **Rule questions** (gifts, conflicts, confidentiality, competence, misrepresentation): the Rules of Conduct.
- **Procedure questions** (who can file, how to get advice, what sanctions exist): the procedures.

Then apply the method in [Working through ethics scenarios](/aicp/lessons/solving-ethics-questions).

## Key terms

- **AICP Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct**: The ethics code that applies to certified planners.
- **Aspirational principles**: The Code's ideals, which aren't by themselves enforceable.
- **Rules of Conduct**: The Code's enforceable standards.
- **Public interest**: The broad community good the Code places first among a planner's responsibilities.
- **Independent professional judgment**: A planner's own honest, competent assessment, not dictated by others' preferences.
- **Ethics Officer**: The AICP staff official who gives advice and administers charges.
- **Informal advice**: Quick, non-binding guidance from the Ethics Officer.
- **Formal advisory opinion**: A written opinion on whether a proposed action complies with the Code.
- **Charge of misconduct**: A formal allegation that a member violated the Rules of Conduct.
- **Sanctions**: Penalties for proven violations, such as reprimand, suspension, or expulsion.

## Real-world examples

**Principle or rule?** A planner's plan gives little attention to the needs of low-income residents. A colleague asks whether the planner "violated the Code." The commitment to social justice is an aspirational principle, so the planner's plan falls short of the Code's ideals, but it can't by itself support a charge of misconduct.

**Asking first.** A planner who works for a city is invited to teach a paid evening course at a university where a professor also consults for developers in the city. Unsure whether this creates a conflict, the planner asks the Ethics Officer for informal advice before accepting.

**A charge from a resident.** A resident who isn't a planner believes a consultant misrepresented data at a public hearing, and files a charge. Because any person can file, the Ethics Officer reviews it, requests a response from the planner, and decides whether to investigate further.

## Exam tips

- Only the **Rules of Conduct** are enforceable; the aspirational principles aren't, on their own.
- Social justice and equity commitments live in the **aspirational principles**.
- **Any person** may file a charge of misconduct.
- Unsure whether something complies? Ask the **Ethics Officer** for advice or a formal advisory opinion.
- Sanctions are reprimand, suspension, or expulsion. AICP can't impose criminal penalties.
- Read the [full Code](https://www.planning.org/ethics/) at least once before the exam. Questions often turn on its exact wording.

## Summary

The AICP Code has aspirational principles, which are ideals and not enforceable on their own, and Rules of Conduct, which are enforceable. The principles cover responsibility to the public (first and foremost, including social justice, equity, full and accurate information, and long-range thinking), to clients and employers (independent judgment within the bounds of the public interest), and to the profession and colleagues. Members unsure of their obligations can get informal advice or request a formal advisory opinion from the Ethics Officer. Any person can file a charge of misconduct, and sanctions include reprimand, suspension, and expulsion.
