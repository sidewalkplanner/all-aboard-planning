## Learning objectives

- Explain what "preparing to plan" involves and why scoping comes before analysis.
- Put the typical steps of a plan-making process in order and explain how they overlap.
- Describe the purpose and contents of an existing conditions report.
- Explain how planners handle tradeoffs among many variables and competing policies.
- Decide when a plan needs a targeted amendment and when it needs a full update.

## Key concepts

### Preparing to plan

Before collecting a single dataset, a planning team needs to answer some basic questions. This "plan for the plan," usually captured in a **scope of work** or work program, sets the project up to succeed:

- **Purpose**: Why are we planning? What decisions will the plan guide?
- **Authority and requirements**: What does state law require (elements, hearings, adoption procedures)? What's the relationship to existing plans?
- **Scope**: What geography, topics, and time horizon (often 20 years for a comprehensive plan)?
- **Stakeholders and engagement**: Who's affected, and how will they be involved? See [Designing public engagement](/aicp/lessons/public-engagement-design).
- **Resources**: budget, staff, consultants, and data.
- **Schedule and decision points**: when will the commission and governing body weigh in?

Defining the plan's purpose, scope, and stakeholders is the most important first step. Skipping it leads to plans that sprawl, stall, or answer the wrong questions.

### The typical sequence

Most planning processes follow a version of the rational model (see [Planning theory](/aicp/lessons/planning-theory)). A common sequence:

1. **Scope the project** (preparing to plan).
2. **Inventory and analyze existing conditions and trends**: demographics, land use, housing, economy, transportation, infrastructure, environment, and hazards.
3. **Identify issues and opportunities** with the community.
4. **Develop a vision and goals.**
5. **Develop alternatives or scenarios** for how the community could grow or change.
6. **Evaluate the alternatives** against the goals and choose a preferred direction.
7. **Draft the plan**: objectives, policies, a future land use map, and an implementation program.
8. **Review and adopt** through public hearings before the planning commission and governing body.
9. **Implement** through regulations, capital investments, programs, and partnerships.
10. **Monitor, evaluate, and update.**

In practice the steps **overlap and loop back**: engagement runs throughout, new data can reopen goals, and evaluation can send the team back to refine alternatives. When an exam question asks for the *first substantive step* after scoping, the usual answer is **inventorying existing conditions**, which comes before drafting goals or maps.

### The existing conditions report

An **existing conditions report** (or community profile) establishes a **shared factual baseline** that residents, staff, and officials can all work from. It typically covers:

- population and household trends and projections;
- land use and development patterns, including vacant and underused land;
- housing supply, condition, costs, and needs;
- economic base and employment;
- transportation networks and travel patterns;
- public facilities and infrastructure capacity;
- **natural resources, constraints, and hazards** (floodplains, steep slopes, wetlands, wildfire risk); and
- fiscal conditions.

Natural constraints and hazard risk belong early in the analysis, because they limit where growth can safely go. A plan's approach to a floodplain should be shaped first by an analysis of the hazard, not by a preferred land use pattern that's later squeezed around it.

:::figure fig-hazards-first | The same site with a floodplain along one edge, planned two ways, with the same number of homes. Hazards first, mapping the risk and then the land use: all the homes sit above a park and trail buffer, and growth stays out of harm's way. Hazards last, with land use drawn first and squeezed around the hazard afterward: three homes end up in the floodplain.
Order changes the outcome: analyze the hazard before drawing the land use, not after.
:::

### Planning as multivariate analysis

A comprehensive plan juggles many variables at once: housing, jobs, transportation, the environment, infrastructure cost, equity, and community character. Changing one affects the others. Adding housing capacity near transit affects school enrollment, traffic, tree canopy, and the tax base.

When goals conflict, for example a housing policy calling for more density in an area where a natural-resource policy limits it, the planner's job isn't to quietly pick a winner. It's to **analyze and surface the tradeoff for decision-makers**: what each option achieves, what it costs, and who's affected. Then elected officials make the value choice, and the plan records it.

:::figure fig-tradeoff | Three illustrative options for the greenfield site in the example below, which contains wildlife habitat. Full build-out: homes spread across the site and the habitat is lost. Clustered: the same number of homes grouped closer together, and the habitat is kept. No change: no new homes, and the habitat is kept.
Staff lay the options side by side with what each gains and loses; the commission and council make the value choice.
:::

### Amend or update?

Plans age. Two kinds of change keep them current:

- A **plan amendment** changes a specific part of the plan, such as redesignating a parcel on the future land use map, adding a new policy, or incorporating a new area plan. Amendments fit changes that are narrow and consistent with the plan's overall direction.
- A **full update** revisits the plan's assumptions, vision, and policies. It's warranted when **conditions have diverged substantially from the plan's assumptions** (growth far faster or slower than projected, a major employer leaving, a disaster, new state mandates) or when the plan's horizon is running out.

Many communities schedule a full review every five to ten years; some states require periodic updates. Frequent piecemeal amendments that pull the plan in different directions are a sign that a full update is overdue.

:::figure fig-growth-drift | A line chart of population growth since a plan was adopted, using the example later in this lesson. The plan assumed 1% a year, or +8% after eight years; actual growth ran 3% a year, or +27%. The gap between the lines is shaded, with a note: time for a full update.
Small annual differences compound: after eight years, the plan's assumption is off by more than three times.
:::

### Strategic plans versus comprehensive plans

A **departmental strategic plan** concentrates on a limited set of priority actions for an organization over a few years, often using tools like SWOT analysis (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats). A **comprehensive plan** covers the whole community, all its physical development topics, over a long horizon. See [Comprehensive plans, elements, and consistency](/aicp/lessons/comprehensive-plans).

## Key terms

- **Scope of work**: The document defining a planning project's purpose, tasks, deliverables, schedule, and budget.
- **Preparing to plan**: Setting purpose, authority, scope, stakeholders, resources, and schedule before analysis begins.
- **Existing conditions report**: A baseline analysis of a community's current conditions and trends.
- **Planning horizon**: The future year a plan looks toward, often 20 years.
- **Alternatives (scenarios)**: Distinct options for future growth or policy that can be compared.
- **Tradeoff**: A situation where advancing one goal means giving up some of another.
- **Plan amendment**: A targeted change to part of an adopted plan.
- **Plan update**: A full revision that revisits a plan's assumptions, vision, and policies.
- **Strategic plan**: A shorter-term plan focused on an organization's priority actions.
- **SWOT analysis**: An assessment of strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats.

## Real-world examples

**Scoping a comprehensive plan update.** A growing suburb's council asks for an updated comprehensive plan. The planning director spends the first two months on scoping: confirming the elements state law requires, setting a 20-year horizon, identifying which topics need consultants (a transportation model, a housing needs assessment), drafting an engagement plan that targets renters and younger residents, and setting three check-ins with the council. The resulting scope becomes the basis for the consultant contract.

**Surfacing a tradeoff.** In a draft plan, the housing element calls for allowing townhouses in a greenfield area to meet projected demand. The natural resources element identifies part of that same area as critical wildlife habitat. Rather than choose, staff present three options with maps and numbers: full development, clustered development that avoids the habitat, and no change. The commission recommends clustering, and the plan explains why.

**When an update is overdue.** A city's plan assumed 1% annual growth, but growth has run at 3% for eight years. The city has adopted 40 site-specific amendments, and the future land use map no longer hangs together. Staff recommend a full update instead of more amendments.

## Exam tips

- After scoping, the first substantive step is **inventorying existing conditions**, before goals or maps.
- Sequencing questions usually follow scoping → existing conditions → issues → vision and goals → alternatives → evaluation → plan → adoption → implementation → monitoring.
- When policies conflict, **surface the tradeoff** for decision-makers; don't resolve it quietly.
- Assumptions far off from reality call for a **full update**, not another amendment.
- Natural constraints and hazards shape the land use approach early; they aren't an afterthought.

## Summary

Planning starts with preparing to plan: defining purpose, authority, scope, stakeholders, resources, and schedule. The typical sequence runs from scoping and existing conditions through issues, vision and goals, alternatives, evaluation, drafting, adoption, implementation, and monitoring, but the steps loop and overlap. An existing conditions report creates a shared baseline, and natural constraints and hazards should shape the analysis early. When goals conflict, surface the tradeoff for decision-makers rather than resolving it quietly. Amend a plan for narrow changes; update it fully when its assumptions no longer hold.
