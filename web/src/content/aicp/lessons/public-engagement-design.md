## Learning objectives

- Explain why planners engage the public and what makes engagement meaningful rather than token.
- Name the rungs and groupings of Arnstein's ladder and the five levels of the IAP2 spectrum.
- Design an engagement strategy by matching purpose, audience, timing, and technique.
- Describe common engagement techniques, including charrettes, visioning, visual preference surveys, and the fishbowl.
- Distinguish a public hearing from a public meeting, and evaluate whether outreach worked.

## Key concepts

### Why engage?

Planning decisions affect people's homes, streets, and livelihoods. Engagement:

- brings in **local knowledge** that data can't capture;
- surfaces **values and priorities**, the choices that are political rather than technical;
- builds **legitimacy** and trust, which makes plans easier to implement;
- is often **legally required** (notice and hearing requirements, federal Title VI obligations); and
- is simply part of the profession's ethical commitment to give people a meaningful role in decisions that affect them.

Engagement is also a research method: talking with residents is how planners come to understand a community.

### Arnstein's ladder of citizen participation

Sherry Arnstein's "A Ladder of Citizen Participation" (1969) ranks participation by how much power residents actually have. From bottom to top:

| Grouping | Rungs |
|---|---|
| **Nonparticipation** | 1. Manipulation · 2. Therapy |
| **Tokenism** | 3. Informing · 4. Consultation · 5. Placation |
| **Citizen power** | 6. Partnership · 7. Delegated power · 8. Citizen control |

The key exam point is that **informing and consultation are forms of tokenism**. Telling residents about a decision already made, or collecting comments that carry no weight, gives the *appearance* of participation without shifting power. **Citizen control** is the top rung.

### The IAP2 spectrum

The International Association for Public Participation's **Spectrum of Public Participation** describes five levels of public influence. Each carries an implied commitment from the agency (the descriptions below are our summaries):

1. **Inform**: the agency provides balanced, timely information so people understand the issue and the decision.
2. **Consult**: the agency asks for feedback and explains afterward how that feedback affected the decision.
3. **Involve**: the agency works directly with the public throughout, so public concerns shape the alternatives.
4. **Collaborate**: the agency and the public develop alternatives and choose a preferred solution together.
5. **Empower**: the public makes the final decision, and the agency carries it out.

No level is always "right." A water main repair may need only *inform*; a neighborhood plan may call for *collaborate*. The ethical failure is **promising one level and delivering a lower one**.

### Designing an engagement strategy

A good strategy, often written as a **communications and engagement plan**, answers:

1. **What decision is being made, and what's actually open?** Be honest about what the public can influence.
2. **Who's affected?** Map stakeholders: residents, businesses, property owners, renters, youth, community organizations, agencies. Pay special attention to groups that are usually underrepresented.
3. **When?** Engage **early**, while problems are being defined and alternatives are still open, not only after a draft is written.
4. **How?** Match techniques to the purpose and audience (see below).
5. **What messages, through which channels and messengers?** Define audiences, key messages, methods, and timing.
6. **How will we report back?** "Closing the loop," showing people how their input was used, is what builds trust.
7. **How will we evaluate it?**

### Common techniques

- **Open house**: drop-in format with stations and staff, good for sharing information and gathering comments.
- **Workshop**: structured, interactive small-group work on a question or map.
- **Charrette**: an **intensive, time-bounded, collaborative design workshop**, often over several days, in which designers work alongside residents and stakeholders with rapid feedback loops. It's well suited to shaping a site or district design while decisions are still open.
- **Visioning**: a process for describing a shared, long-range picture of the community's desired future.
- **Visual preference survey**: participants rate images of buildings, streets, or places, which helps turn vague preferences ("character") into design direction.
- **Surveys**: statistically valid random-sample surveys measure how opinion is distributed across a population; open online surveys gather ideas but are self-selected. See [Research design and data sources](/aicp/lessons/research-design-and-data).
- **Focus groups**: small, facilitated discussions for depth and nuance.
- **Advisory committees**: standing groups that guide a project over time.
- **Fishbowl**: a small group discusses an issue in an inner circle while a larger audience observes, sometimes with an open chair for audience members to join.
- **Pop-up engagement**: going to where people already are (markets, transit stops, festivals, schools).
- **Online tools**: interactive maps, comment platforms, and virtual meetings, which extend reach but miss people without reliable internet.
- **Participatory budgeting**: residents directly decide how to spend part of a public budget, at the upper end of the spectrum.

### Public hearings versus public meetings

- A **public hearing** is a formal proceeding, usually required by law, before a decision-making body. Testimony becomes part of the **official record** that supports (and can be used to challenge) the decision. Notice requirements are strict.
- A **public meeting**, workshop, or open house is less formal and exists to share information and gather ideas. It usually doesn't create a legal record.

Hearings are a poor tool for shaping a plan: they come late, favor confident speakers, and invite position-taking. Use them for what they're for, and do the real engagement earlier.

### Evaluating outreach

Ask both *process* and *outcome* questions:

- **Who participated?** Compare participants with the community's demographics (tenure, age, race and ethnicity, language, income, neighborhood). If renters and young families are missing, add targeted outreach to reach them.
- **Did input change anything?** Can you point to specific changes in the plan?
- **Did participants understand the choices and feel heard?** Short exit surveys help.

The most reliable single indicator of effective outreach to an underrepresented community is **participation that reflects that community**.

## Key terms

- **Arnstein's ladder**: An eight-rung ranking of participation from nonparticipation through tokenism to citizen power.
- **Tokenism**: Participation that offers a voice without real influence (informing, consultation, placation).
- **IAP2 spectrum**: Five levels of public influence: inform, consult, involve, collaborate, empower.
- **Stakeholder mapping**: Identifying who is affected by or can affect a decision.
- **Communications plan**: A plan defining audiences, messages, methods, and timing.
- **Charrette**: An intensive, time-limited, collaborative design workshop.
- **Visioning**: A process for describing a community's desired long-range future.
- **Visual preference survey**: A technique in which participants rate images of development types.
- **Fishbowl**: A format where a small group discusses while a larger group observes.
- **Public hearing**: A formal, legally noticed proceeding that creates a record for a decision.
- **Closing the loop**: Reporting back to participants how their input was used.

## Real-world examples

**Starting early.** A city begins a corridor plan not with a draft but with a question: "What would make this street work better for you?" Staff hold pop-up events at bus stops, a grocery store, and a high school, in the corridor's three main languages. The resulting problem statement, built with residents, shapes the alternatives. By the time the formal hearing arrives, few people are surprised by the recommendation.

**A charrette for a key site.** For a vacant former mall, the city runs a four-day charrette on site. Designers sketch options each morning, residents react each afternoon, and the team revises overnight. By the final evening, participants have converged on a concept for a new street grid, a central green, and housing above shops.

**Fixing lopsided participation.** After the first round of engagement on a housing plan, staff compare participants with census data and find that renters are 55% of households but only 15% of participants. They partner with tenant organizations, hold meetings in apartment community rooms at different times, and offer childcare and food. Renter participation in the second round rises substantially.

## Summary

Engagement brings in local knowledge, surfaces values, builds legitimacy, and is often legally required. Arnstein's ladder warns that informing and consultation can be tokenism, and the IAP2 spectrum asks planners to be honest about the level of influence they promise, from inform to empower. Good strategies start early, map stakeholders, match techniques to purpose (charrettes for design, random-sample surveys for measurement, pop-ups for reach), and close the loop. Hearings create the legal record but are the wrong tool for shaping a plan. Judge outreach by whether participants reflect the community and whether their input changed the result.
