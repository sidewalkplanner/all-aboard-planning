## Learning objectives

- Apply core urban design principles and the lessons of observational research on public space.
- Explain placemaking and the principles of Crime Prevention Through Environmental Design (CPTED).
- Describe design review tools, including context-sensitive standards and the transect.
- Explain what National Register listing does and doesn't do, and walk through Section 106 review.
- Distinguish the four treatments in the Secretary of the Interior's Standards, and explain local historic districts and the rehabilitation tax credit.

## Key concepts

### Principles of urban design

Urban design shapes the physical setting for public life: the relationships among buildings, streets, and open spaces. Recurring principles:

- **Human scale**: buildings, signs, and spaces sized and detailed for people on foot.
- **Enclosure**: streets and plazas feel like outdoor rooms when building heights are in proportion to the space's width.
- **Connectivity (permeability)**: short blocks and connected streets give people many route choices.
- **Legibility**: a clear structure of paths, edges, districts, nodes, and landmarks makes a place easy to understand (Kevin Lynch's elements; see [Patterns of human settlement](/aicp/lessons/urban-form-and-settlement)).
- **Active edges**: ground floors with doors, windows, and activity make streets feel safe and interesting. Blank walls and parking lots don't.
- **Mix and diversity** of uses, building types, and people.
- **A quality public realm**: sidewalks, trees, lighting, seating, and public spaces that invite people to stay.

### Learning from how people use space

Observational research shaped modern practice. **William H. Whyte**'s studies of New York plazas (*The Social Life of Small Urban Spaces*, 1980) found that successful plazas have plenty of **sittable space**, sun, trees, water, food vendors, and a strong connection to the street. People attract people. Danish architect **Jan Gehl** similarly documented how design at eye level affects whether people linger in public spaces.

### Placemaking

**Placemaking** is a collaborative approach to shaping **public spaces around the community's identity, needs, and activities**. It starts with how people want to use a place rather than with a fixed design. Related is **tactical urbanism**: short-term, low-cost interventions (painted curb extensions, pop-up plazas, temporary bike lanes) that test ideas before permanent investment.

### Crime Prevention Through Environmental Design (CPTED)

**CPTED** uses design to reduce opportunities for crime and increase feelings of safety. Its core principles:

- **Natural surveillance**: arrange windows, lighting, and activity so people can see and be seen.
- **Natural access control**: guide movement with entrances, paths, and landscaping.
- **Territorial reinforcement**: define public, semi-private, and private space so ownership is clear.
- **Maintenance** (and activity support): well-kept places signal that someone cares.

CPTED is about design; it doesn't include criminal justice measures such as sentencing. Apply it carefully so it doesn't become a pretext for excluding people from public space.

### Design review and standards

Communities guide design through **design guidelines** (advisory or discretionary) and **design standards** (objective, enforceable), administered by staff or a design review board.

- **Context-sensitive standards** calibrate requirements to the setting, such as a historic main street, a suburban corridor, or a rural crossroads, rather than applying one rule everywhere.
- **Form-based codes** and the **transect**, which organizes a rural-to-urban gradient, are the most systematic version. See [Growth management and innovative land use tools](/aicp/lessons/growth-management-and-innovative-tools).
- **Objective standards** (measurable rules like "at least 60% of the ground-floor facade must be windows") are increasingly preferred over subjective ones ("compatible with the neighborhood"), because they're predictable and harder to use to block housing.

### Historic preservation: the federal framework

The **National Historic Preservation Act (NHPA)** of 1966 created the core system:

- The **National Register of Historic Places** is the official federal list of districts, sites, buildings, structures, and objects significant in American history, architecture, archaeology, engineering, or culture. Properties are generally at least 50 years old and must meet one or more criteria: association with significant **events**, association with significant **people**, distinctive **design or construction**, or the potential to yield important **information** (usually archaeological).
- **Listing is largely honorific for private owners.** It does **not by itself restrict what a private owner may do** with their property using private funds. It does make the property eligible for certain incentives and triggers review of *federal* actions affecting it.
- **State Historic Preservation Offices (SHPOs)** and **Tribal Historic Preservation Offices (THPOs)** run surveys, nominations, and review. Local governments can become **Certified Local Governments** to take a formal role and qualify for certain grants.

:::checkpoint cp:design-register-criteria

### Section 106 review

**Section 106** of the NHPA requires **federal agencies to take into account the effects of their undertakings** (projects they fund, permit, or carry out) on properties **listed in or eligible for** the National Register, and to consult. The basic steps:

1. Initiate the process and identify **consulting parties** (the SHPO or THPO, local governments, tribes, the public).
2. **Identify** historic properties in the project area.
3. **Assess** whether the effects are adverse.
4. **Resolve** adverse effects by avoiding, minimizing, or mitigating them, usually documented in a memorandum of agreement.

Like NEPA, Section 106 is **procedural**: it requires consideration and consultation, not preservation. (Transportation projects also face a separate federal rule that restricts the use of historic sites and parks when a prudent and feasible alternative exists.) <!-- VERIFY: this is Section 4(f) of the Department of Transportation Act of 1966; confirm wording before naming it. -->

### Local historic districts

The strongest protection for historic buildings is **local**. A local preservation ordinance creates a **historic preservation commission** and designates landmarks and districts. Changes to designated properties usually require a **certificate of appropriateness**, reviewed against adopted **design guidelines**. Many ordinances also regulate or delay **demolition**.

:::checkpoint cp:design-local-strongest

### The Secretary of the Interior's Standards

The **Secretary of the Interior's Standards for the Treatment of Historic Properties** describe four treatments:

| Treatment | Approach |
|---|---|
| **Preservation** | Sustain the existing form and materials through maintenance and repair |
| **Rehabilitation** | Make possible a compatible **new use** while preserving the features that convey historic character. It's the most common treatment. |
| **Restoration** | Accurately depict the property as it appeared at a particular period, removing later features |
| **Reconstruction** | Re-create a vanished or non-surviving building or feature with new construction |

:::checkpoint cp:design-rehab

### The federal rehabilitation tax credit

The federal **Historic Rehabilitation Tax Credit** provides a **20% credit** for the qualified costs of a certified rehabilitation of an **income-producing certified historic structure**, reviewed against the Standards for Rehabilitation. Owner-occupied homes don't qualify for the federal credit, though some state credits cover them. <!-- VERIFY: confirm the credit remains 20% and the current rules on claiming it (spread over five years since 2017), and that the 10% non-historic credit remains repealed. -->

## Key terms

- **Human scale**: Design proportioned to people on foot.
- **Active edge**: A building frontage with doors, windows, and activity facing the street.
- **Placemaking**: Collaboratively shaping public spaces around community identity and use.
- **Tactical urbanism**: Short-term, low-cost interventions to test changes to public space.
- **CPTED**: Crime Prevention Through Environmental Design.
- **Natural surveillance**: Design that lets people see and be seen.
- **Design guidelines**: Advisory or discretionary design criteria.
- **Objective design standards**: Measurable, non-discretionary design rules.
- **National Register of Historic Places**: The official federal list of significant historic properties.
- **Section 106**: The NHPA requirement that federal agencies consider effects on historic properties and consult.
- **SHPO/THPO**: State and Tribal Historic Preservation Offices.
- **Certificate of appropriateness**: Approval for changes to a property in a local historic district.
- **Rehabilitation**: A treatment adapting a historic property to a compatible new use while preserving its character.
- **Historic Rehabilitation Tax Credit**: A 20% federal credit for certified rehabilitation of income-producing historic buildings.

## Real-world examples

**Redesigning a plaza.** A downtown plaza sits empty most days. Staff spend a week observing it, Whyte-style: there's almost no seating, no shade, and a blank wall on one side. A tactical pilot adds movable chairs, a food truck, and planters. Use triples, and the permanent redesign keeps those features.

**Section 106 on a bridge project.** A state DOT plans to replace a bridge using federal funds. The old bridge is eligible for the National Register. Through Section 106 consultation with the SHPO and a local historical society, the agency documents the bridge, reuses its railings on the new bridge, and installs interpretive panels, all recorded in a memorandum of agreement.

**Tax credits for a warehouse.** A developer converts a certified historic warehouse into apartments with ground-floor retail. The rehabilitation follows the Secretary's Standards for Rehabilitation, which qualifies the income-producing project for the 20% federal credit and a state credit, and makes the project financially feasible.

## Exam tips

- National Register listing is **largely honorific** for private owners and doesn't by itself restrict them.
- Section 106 requires federal agencies to **consider effects and consult**. It doesn't require preservation.
- The Secretary's Standards cover **preservation, rehabilitation, restoration, and reconstruction**.
- Local historic districts work through **certificates of appropriateness** against design guidelines.
- The 20% federal credit is for **income-producing** certified historic buildings.
- CPTED principles include natural surveillance, access control, territorial reinforcement, and maintenance, not criminal penalties.

## Summary

Good urban design is human-scaled, enclosed, connected, legible, active at the edges, mixed, and centered on a strong public realm. Whyte's and Gehl's research show that seating, sun, food, and activity make spaces work. Placemaking starts with how people use space, and tactical urbanism tests ideas cheaply. CPTED relies on natural surveillance, access control, territorial reinforcement, and maintenance. National Register listing is largely honorific for private owners but triggers Section 106 review of federal undertakings, which is procedural. Local districts, through certificates of appropriateness, provide the strongest protection. The Secretary's Standards define preservation, rehabilitation, restoration, and reconstruction, and the 20% federal credit rewards certified rehabilitation of income-producing historic buildings.
